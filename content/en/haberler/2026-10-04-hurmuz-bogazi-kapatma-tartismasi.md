Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an assessment by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—suggest the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; while carrying modest volumes now, these alternative pathways could permanently erode the strait's geopolitical value in the long run. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for an extended period might produce consequences more dangerous than reopening it.

By contrast, the hardline Kayhan draws a different picture: in a "war day 218" report, it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall when the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, the market dismissed these statements; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is ready with the International Energy Agency to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will only provide short-term relief, as global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which the Persian Gulf opens to the sea and through which a significant portion of world petroleum shipments pass; because its waters are partly bounded by Iran's coasts, Tehran has strategic control over it. During wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have pushed oil prices markedly above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [an earlier report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the paper, even if the several million barrels daily rerouted from the south seems minor now, should this flow become permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this would constitute, in the newspaper's view, a larger strategic loss than reopening the strait.
