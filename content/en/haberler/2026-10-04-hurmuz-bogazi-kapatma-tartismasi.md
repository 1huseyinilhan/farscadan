Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate conservative outlet Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become costlier. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—suggest that the strait's blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article highlights the growing entrenchment of "circumvention" routes: pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these routes currently carry only modest volumes of oil, they could permanently erode the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for a prolonged period may produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

By contrast, the hardline daily Kayhan presents a different framing. In a report titled "day 218 of the war," Kayhan notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall "once the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, the market ignores these statements; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from strategic reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. Kayhan argues that this situation proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready, alongside the International Energy Agency, to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief; global supply will structurally remain depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open sea through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass; its partial encirclement by Iran's coastlines gives Tehran strategic monitoring capability. In wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's imposed restrictions have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to an earlier report on the [tanker strike incident](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security event in the strait becomes a new point of reckoning for both political factions in Tehran and global energy markets.

The chief risk highlighted by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while the few million barrels of oil bypassing through the south currently appear insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper views this as an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
