Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a debated question in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 compared to $70 before the war indicate that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights the increasing entrenchment of "workaround" routes—such as pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these alternative routes currently carry small volumes of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period might produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan paints a different picture: in a report headlined "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated his claim that "oil prices will fall when the war ends" for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. The market has ignored Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, and despite the US announcing a 40 million barrel strategic reserve release, Brent crude climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is putting pressure on the US economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready to release emergency reserves alongside the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, as global supply will structurally remain depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran a strategic control opportunity. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions implemented by Iran have driven oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to the [earlier story of a tanker being struck](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk highlighted by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the newspaper, while the few million barrels of oil detouring south may seem insignificant now, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years—something the newspaper argues is an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
