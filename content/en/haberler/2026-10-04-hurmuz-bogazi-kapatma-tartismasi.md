Whether Iran's Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed remains a contested question in Tehran's press. According to an assessment published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

By Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—are evidence that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article notes that "workarounds," such as pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait, are becoming increasingly entrenched; while these routes currently carry small volumes, they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. For this reason, the newspaper suggests that keeping the strait closed for extended periods could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan draws a different frame: In a report headlined "the war's 218th day," Kayhan notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and a U.S. stockpile release of 40 million barrels, Brent crude climbed back to $103, Kayhan reports. To Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union and the International Energy Agency stand ready to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief and global supply would remain structurally constrained.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway extending from the Persian Gulf to open ocean through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass; its partial enclosure by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic oversight capability. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have pushed oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [an earlier report about a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The chief risk Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the newspaper, while a few million barrels daily routed around the strait now seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, the strait's deterrent power could be weakened for years; this too is a larger strategic loss than opening the strait, the newspaper argues.
