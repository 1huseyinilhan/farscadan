Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic faces disruption and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 compared to pre-war levels of $70 suggest that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workaround" routes—pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these alternatives currently move small volumes, they risk permanently reducing the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. For this reason, Khabaronline argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames the issue differently. In a report titled "Day 218 of the war," Kayhan notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends—yet they remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. The article states that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, the market has dismissed these statements. Even after the U.S. released 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103. For Kayhan, this proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready to release emergency reserves alongside the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only temporary relief while global supply remains structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. Its partial enclosure by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control. During wartime, security risks in the strait and Iranian-imposed restrictions have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to [an earlier report about a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political factions in Tehran and global energy markets.

The core risk Khabaronline identifies is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While the few million barrels flowing south currently seem marginal, the article warns that if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; to the newspaper, this represents a greater strategic loss than opening it.
