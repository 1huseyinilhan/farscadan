Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; much of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit costs have increased. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—indicate that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article notes that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly relying on "workarounds" such as pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry small volumes, over the long term they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, keeping the strait closed for an extended period may produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the issue differently. In a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices would fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply, the market has discounted those statements, and despite a 40 million barrel strategic petroleum reserve release, Brent crude returned to $103. According to Kayhan, this shows that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing to release emergency stockpiles together with the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief, and global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of world oil is shipped. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control over it. In wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion is connected to a previous article about a [tanker being struck](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security event in the strait becomes a new point of reckoning for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, even though the few million barrels currently being diverted from the south appears small, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this would represent a larger strategic loss than opening the strait.
