Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as a significant lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the conflict—suggest the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workarounds" such as pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; though these currently move small volumes, they risk permanently diminishing the strait's geopolitical value over time. For this reason, the paper argues that keeping the strait closed long-term could pose a greater strategic threat than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the issue differently: in a report titled "218th day of war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the conflict. The paper argues that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and despite a 40-million-barrel strategic reserve release, Brent oil climbed back to $103, proving that Iran's pressure on the strait has strained the U.S. economy. Kayhan also cites former International Energy Agency official Neil Atkinson, who argues that emergency reserves offer only short-term relief and global supply will remain structurally constrained.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between the Persian Gulf and the open sea through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. Iran's position on its coast gives Tehran strategic control over this chokepoint. During the conflict, security risks and Iranian restrictions have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to [an earlier story on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline highlights is the normalization of alternative routes. Though only a few million barrels now detour southward, if this flow becomes routine, it could permanently weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper considers this a greater strategic loss than opening the strait altogether.
