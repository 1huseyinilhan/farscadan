Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a debated question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become costlier. The paper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel, compared to $70 before the conflict, are evidence that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article notes the increasing entrenchment of "workaround" routes: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iraq's pipelines, and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. Although these alternative routes currently carry only small volumes of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. For this reason, Khabaronline argues that keeping the strait closed for an extended period may produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan paints a different picture. In a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it writes that Trump has been repeating for seven months that "oil prices will fall after the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than prewar levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, markets have ignored these statements; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels of reserves, Brent oil climbed back to $103. Kayhan argues this proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also cites a former International Energy Agency official, Neil Atkinson, saying that the EU and IEA releasing emergency reserves would only provide temporary relief and global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which significant portions of global oil shipments pass, connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control over traffic. During conflict, security risks and Iranian restrictions in the strait have pushed oil prices significantly above prewar levels. This debate links to an [earlier report](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/) about a tanker strike: every security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

Khabaronline's central risk is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the paper, although a few million barrels daily routing southward now seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years—a larger strategic loss than opening the strait, in the paper's view.
