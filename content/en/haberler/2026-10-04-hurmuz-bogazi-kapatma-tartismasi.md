Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an assessment published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100, compared to $70 before the war, are evidence that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq's pipeline workarounds—such as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait—are becoming increasingly established: while these routes currently carry small volumes, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. According to the newspaper, this is why keeping the strait closed for a prolonged period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan presents a different frame: in a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices would fall when the war ends, but prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply and the U.S. announcement of 40 million barrels in reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this shows that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, working with the International Energy Agency, stands ready to release emergency reserves. However, according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this provides only short-term relief; global supply will remain structurally weak.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass, connecting the Persian Gulf to open waters. Because it is partially bordered by Iran's coastline, Tehran has strategic control over it. Under war conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran-imposed restrictions have raised oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This debate connects to the earlier report on [a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation point for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The main risk Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while the few million barrels that currently bypass the strait southward may seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper views this as an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
