Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a subject of debate in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the bulk of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper defines this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices above $100 per barrel, compared to $70 before the war, indicate that the strait's blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights the growing entrenchment of "workaround" routes—pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these alternatives currently carry small quantities of oil, over the long term they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan frames the situation differently: In a report headlined "war day 218," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the conflict. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply and the US announcement of 40 million barrels in reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. Kayhan argues this proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the US economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, working with the International Energy Agency, stands ready to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this provides only short-term relief—global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, through which a substantial share of the world's oil passes. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's restrictions have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [an earlier report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a fresh calculation for both Tehran's political camps and global energy markets.

The real risk highlighted by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While a few million barrels bypassing southward currently seem marginal, if that flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years—which the newspaper sees as a bigger strategic loss than opening it.
