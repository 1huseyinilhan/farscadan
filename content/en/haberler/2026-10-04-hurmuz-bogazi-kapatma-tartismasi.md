Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a debated question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the conflict—indicate that the strait blockade has achieved some effect to a certain degree. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry modest volumes of oil, they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the situation differently. In a report titled "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated his claim that "oil prices will fall when the war ends" for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise to increase supply, the market has dismissed these statements. Despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103, Kayhan argues—evidence that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the American economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, working with the International Energy Agency, is prepared to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief and global supply would continue to remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway that connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and then to the open ocean, through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastlines gives Tehran strategic control over this chokepoint. During wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion is linked to [a previous report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a point of recalculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets alike.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While the newspaper acknowledges that the several million barrels daily taking detours appear marginal now, the entrenchment of this flow could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a loss the newspaper considers more catastrophic than opening the passage.
