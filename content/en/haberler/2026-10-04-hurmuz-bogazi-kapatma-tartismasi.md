Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a debated question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither entirely open nor entirely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy passage has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to around $70 before the war—suggest that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the piece notes that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq's pipelines, along with ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait, are increasingly becoming established "workarounds": while these routes carry only small quantities of oil for now, in the long term they could permanently erode the strait's geopolitical value. According to the newspaper, this is why keeping the strait closed for an extended period may produce a more dangerous result than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames the issue differently. In a report titled "War Day 218," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that "oil prices will fall when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, the market has not heeded these statements; even with a U.S. announcement of releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent oil surged back to $103. According to Kayhan, this proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready, together with the International Energy Agency, to release emergency stockpiles, but according to former IEA director Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief and global supply would remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway leading from the Persian Gulf to open sea, through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass. Because it is partly bordered by Iran's coastline, it gives Tehran strategic control over traffic. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's imposed restrictions have pushed oil prices significantly above prewar levels. This debate connects to [an earlier story about a tanker being hit](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a fresh calculation for both Tehran's political camps and global energy markets.

The primary risk highlighted by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while the detour of a few million barrels bypassing the strait seems minor today, if this flow becomes permanent it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper argues this would be a greater strategic loss than opening the strait outright.
