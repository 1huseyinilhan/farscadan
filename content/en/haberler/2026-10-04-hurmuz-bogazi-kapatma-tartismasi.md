Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed remains a question debated in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel, compared to pre-war levels of $70, indicate that the strait closure is working to some degree. However, the article notes the emergence of increasingly permanent "workaround" routes, including pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these routes currently carry small amounts of oil, they could permanently reduce the geopolitical value of the strait over the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for extended periods could produce more dangerous consequences than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan presents a different framework: in a report headlined "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices would fall after the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, the market ignores these statements, and despite the U.S. announcing a release of 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103 per barrel. For Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready with the International Energy Agency to release emergency stockpiles, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief; global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open sea and is the passage for a significant share of the world's oil shipments. Its partial encirclement by Iranian coastlines gives Tehran strategic control over it. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have driven oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This debate connects to a previous news story about a tanker strike: every security incident in the strait becomes a subject of political recalculation for both camps in Tehran and for global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, even though a few million barrels per day flowing south currently appears minor, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this would represent a larger strategic loss than opening it, in the newspaper's view.
