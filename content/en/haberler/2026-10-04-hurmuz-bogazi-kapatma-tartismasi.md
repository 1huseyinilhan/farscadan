Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper defines this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100, compared to $70 before the war, are evidence that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq have increasingly established "workaround" routes such as pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry small amounts of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. For this reason, according to the newspaper, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous result than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan presents a different frame: in a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it writes that Trump has repeated his claim that oil prices will fall when the war ends for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, the market disregarded these announcements; despite the US releasing 40 million barrels from its reserves, Brent crude again rose to $103. According to Kayhan, this situation is evidence that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the US economy.

Kayhan also notes that the European Union, together with the International Energy Agency, is prepared to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, and global supply would continue to remain structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass; because its waters are partially surrounded by Iran's coastline, it gives Tehran strategic control. Under war conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have raised oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This debate is also linked to the [previous report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a subject of recalculation for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The key risk that Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, even though the current flow of a few million barrels bypassing from the south may appear insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come; this would be an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait, in the newspaper's view.
