Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or closed has been a subject of debate in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative outlet Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic has stalled, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel compared to $70 before the conflict suggests that the blockade of the strait is working to some extent. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly establishing "workarounds" such as pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; though these routes currently carry modest volumes of oil, they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over time. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for a prolonged period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames the issue differently. In a report titled "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months his claim that oil prices will fall when the fighting ends, yet prices remain $30 per barrel higher than before the war. It observes that markets have ignored Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, and even after the U.S. announced a 40 million-barrel stockpile release, Brent crude climbed back to $103. For Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the American economy.

Kayhan also cites information that the European Union and the International Energy Agency are prepared to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would only provide short-term relief, as global supply would remain structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to open ocean, through which a significant portion of the world's oil transits; Iran's position along its shores gives Tehran strategic control over this passage. During wartime, the security risks in the strait and the restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices substantially above pre-conflict levels. This debate connects to [an earlier report on the attack on a tanker](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a matter of fresh calculation for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The core risk that Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While the newspaper initially makes light of the few million barrels bypassing the strait, it stresses that if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this represents, in its view, an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait altogether.
