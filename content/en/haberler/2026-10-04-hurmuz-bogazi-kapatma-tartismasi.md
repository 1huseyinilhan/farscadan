Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contentious question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 compared to the pre-war level of $70 indicate that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry small quantities of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan presents a different frame. In a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that "oil prices will fall when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. It points out that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise to increase supply, the market has not heeded these statements, and Brent crude has climbed back to $103 despite a U.S. announcement of 40 million barrels in stockpile releases. According to Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is prepared to release emergency reserves in coordination with the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would only provide short-term relief, and global supply will continue to face structural constraints.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean and carries a significant portion of the world's oil shipments; its partial encirclement by Iranian shores gives Tehran strategic oversight. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and the restrictions Iran has implemented have driven oil prices notably above pre-war levels. This debate is linked to [the earlier report of a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a new point of calculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The main risk that Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while the several million barrels of oil currently detouring from the south may seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper argues this would be a greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
