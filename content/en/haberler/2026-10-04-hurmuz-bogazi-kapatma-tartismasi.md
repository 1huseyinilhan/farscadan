Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed remains a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative outlet Khabaronline, the status of the strait is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic faces disruption and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this situation as an important leverage point in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 a barrel compared to around $70 before the war suggest that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly using "workaround" methods—pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these routes currently carry modest volumes of oil, they could permanently diminish the geopolitical value of the strait in the long run. For this reason, keeping the strait closed for an extended period might produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline outlet Kayhan frames the issue differently. In a report titled "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months his claim that "oil prices will fall when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Kayhan points out that the market has not heeded Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, and that despite a U.S. announcement of releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, working with the International Energy Agency, is prepared to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, as global supply will remain structurally constrained.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway where ships exit the Persian Gulf into open ocean and where a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass. Iran's geographic position along its shores gives Tehran strategic control over this chokepoint. During wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices substantially above pre-war levels. This discussion is linked to [a previous report on the tanker incident](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security event in the strait becomes a fresh calculus for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The main risk that Khabaronline underscores is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While the modest volumes of oil bypassing the strait through the south may seem insignificant for now, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a greater strategic loss than opening it, the newspaper argues.
