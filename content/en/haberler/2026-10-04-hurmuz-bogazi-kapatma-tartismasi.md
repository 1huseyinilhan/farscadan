Whether the Strait of Hormuz is completely open or closed has been a debated question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices above $100—compared to $70 before the war—suggest that the blockade of the strait has worked to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: though these alternative routes currently carry only modest amounts of oil, over time they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for a long time may produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan takes a different approach: in its 218th day of war report, it says Trump has repeated for seven months that "oil will fall when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Kayhan notes that Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply has not convinced the market; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from its reserves, Brent oil rebounded to $103. According to Kayhan, this proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is squeezing the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, working with the International Energy Agency, is preparing to release emergency reserves—but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief, and global supply will continue to face structural constraints.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which a significant portion of the world's oil passes, flowing from the Persian Gulf to open waters. Iran's location along its shores gives Tehran strategic control over this passage. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to a [previous report on a tanker attack](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The main risk highlighted by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the newspaper, even if only a few million barrels are currently being diverted south of the strait, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years—a strategic loss greater than simply opening the strait would be.
