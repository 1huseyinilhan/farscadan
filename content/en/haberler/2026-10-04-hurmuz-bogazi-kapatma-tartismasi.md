Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a debated question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic is disrupted and energy transit is costlier. The newspaper describes this as a significant lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices above $100, compared to $70 before the war, suggest that the blockade of the strait is working to some degree. However, the article notes that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly settling into "workaround" routes such as pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry small quantities of oil, they could permanently reduce the geopolitical value of the strait over the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues that keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames the issue differently: in a report titled "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has been repeating for seven months that oil prices will fall when the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. It points out that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and the U.S. announcement of 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this shows that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing with the International Energy Agency to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief; global supply is likely to remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean and carries a significant portion of the world's oil shipments; its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's imposed restrictions have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate is connected to an earlier report on the [tanker attack](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a fresh calculation for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The main risk that Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels of oil flowing around from the south seem insignificant now, if that flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper considers this a greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
