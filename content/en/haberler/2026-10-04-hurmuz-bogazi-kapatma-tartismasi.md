Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a topic of debate in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel—compared to $70 before the war—are evidence that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article notes the entrenchment of "workaround" routes, including pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; while these routes carry only small quantities of oil for now, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long term. For this reason, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it, according to the newspaper.

The hardline Kayhan takes a different frame: in a report titled "war day 218," it notes that Trump has repeated his claim that "oil prices will fall when the war ends" for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, the market has not heeded these statements; even after the US announced releases from its 40-million-barrel strategic reserve, Brent oil has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is forcing the U.S. economy to strain.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is prepared to release emergency stockpiles jointly with the International Energy Agency, but according to Neil Atkinson, a former IEA official cited in the article, this will provide only short-term relief, as global supply will structurally remain low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway running from the Persian Gulf to the open sea and carries a significant share of global oil shipments. Iran's partial encirclement of the strait gives Tehran strategic control over it. Under wartime conditions, security risks at the strait and the restrictions Iran has imposed have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to an earlier news report about [a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident at the strait becomes a fresh calculus for both political camps in Tehran and for global energy markets.

The key risk underscored by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, although the few million barrels detouring south appear insignificant today, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a strategic loss greater than reopening it, in the newspaper's assessment.
