Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic is disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The outlet describes this as an important leverage point in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 a barrel compared to $70 before the conflict suggests that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workaround" routes, including pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; though these currently carry only modest quantities of oil, over the long term they could permanently degrade the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, Khabaronline argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the situation differently. In a report titled "218 days of war," it notes that Trump repeated for seven months that oil prices would fall once the conflict ended, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. The outlet argues that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply and even a U.S. announcement of releasing 40 million barrels, Brent crude climbed back to $103, proving that the market isn't convinced. Kayhan sees this as evidence that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing, together with the International Energy Agency, to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will only provide short-term relief, as the global supply shortage will structurally persist.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass; Iran's coastline partially surrounds it, giving Tehran strategic control. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has applied have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [an earlier report on a tanker attack](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/), since each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political factions in Tehran and global energy markets.

The main risk Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While a few million barrels daily detoured from the south may seem modest now, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the outlet argues this represents a larger strategic loss than opening the strait.
