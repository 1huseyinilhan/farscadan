Whether Iran is keeping the Strait of Hormuz fully open or fully closed has been a debated question in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the conflict—indicate that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article notes that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these routes currently transport only small quantities of oil, they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over time. For this reason, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the situation differently. In a report titled "Day 218 of the conflict," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the conflict. It states that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply, markets are dismissing these statements; despite a U.S. announcement of releasing 40 million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing to release emergency fuel reserves alongside the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief while global supply will remain structurally weak and unable to recover quickly.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass; Iran's position along its coasts gives Tehran strategic control over the chokepoint. During wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices well above prewar levels. This debate connects to [an earlier Farsçadan report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation for both Tehran's political camps and global energy markets.

The main risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels daily flowing south of the strait may seem insignificant now, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a greater strategic loss than opening the strait, in the newspaper's view.
