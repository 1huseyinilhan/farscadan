Whether the Hormuz Strait is completely open or completely closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper defines this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—indicate that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq have increasingly adopted "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: though these routes currently carry small amounts of oil, in the long term they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, Khabaronline argues, keeping the strait closed for extended periods could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan paints a different frame: in a "day 218 of war" report, it writes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. It states that despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply, markets have ignored these statements, and despite a U.S. announcement of 40 million barrels in reserves, Brent oil rose back to $103. According to Kayhan, this proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is prepared to release emergency stocks in coordination with the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will only provide short-term relief while global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Hormuz Strait is a narrow waterway through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass, connecting the Persian Gulf to open sea. Its partial encirclement by Iran's shores gives Tehran strategic control over passage. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's imposed restrictions have pushed oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to the [earlier news about a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a subject of recalculation for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The main risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, though the several million barrels currently bypassing to the south may seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this would represent, in Khabaronline's view, an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
