Whether the Strait of Hormuz is exactly open or closed has been a subject of debate in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's condition is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; most traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper describes this as an important leverage point in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100, compared to $70 before the war, are evidence that the strait blockade is working to some extent. However, the article points to the establishment of "bypass" routes through pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry limited quantities of oil, their permanent establishment could eventually reduce the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. According to the newspaper, this is why keeping the strait closed for an extended period might produce more dangerous consequences than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan, meanwhile, presents a different frame: in a report headlined "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated his claim that "oil prices will fall when the war ends" for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. It points out that the market ignores Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply, and that Brent crude returned to $103 despite the US announcing a 40-million-barrel strategic release. According to Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the US economy.

Kayhan also notes that the European Union stands ready, alongside the International Energy Agency, to release emergency stockpiles. However, according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson cited in the article, this will provide only short-term relief, while global supply will continue to remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open sea, through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass. Its partial enclosure by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control capabilities. Under war conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions applied by Iran have pushed oil prices significantly above pre-war levels. This debate also connects to an earlier story about a [tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation point for both political factions in Tehran and for global energy markets.

The main risk emphasized by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the newspaper, while the flow of a few million barrels daily rerouted through the south may seem insignificant now, the permanent establishment of this flow could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—which the newspaper views as an even greater strategic loss than opening the strait would be.
