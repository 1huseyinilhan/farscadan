Whether the Strait of Hormuz is truly open or closed has been a subject of debate in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor completely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the conflict—suggest that the strait blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article points out the growing entrenchment of "workaround" routes through Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq's pipelines, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: while these routes currently carry small volumes, in the long term they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan presents a different framing: in a report headlined "Day 218 of the war," it writes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices would fall once the conflict ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promise of increased supply and the market's dismissal of these statements, and despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is squeezing the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union, together with the International Energy Agency, is prepared to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, and global supply will continue to remain structurally constrained.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between the Persian Gulf and the open sea through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass. Its partial proximity to Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate is linked to an earlier report about [a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a subject of calculation for both Tehran's political camps and global energy markets.

The primary risk emphasized by Khabaronline is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels of oil routed southward may seem insignificant now, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—which the newspaper argues would be a greater strategic loss than opening the strait.
