Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the Moderate conservative outlet Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the bulk of traffic has been disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to roughly $70 before the war—suggest that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq have increasingly established "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; although these currently transport small volumes, they could permanently erode the strait's geopolitical value over time. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for a long period could yield a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames things differently. In a report headlined "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's pledge to increase supply, the market has dismissed these statements; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels of reserves, Brent crude has climbed back to $103. For Kayhan, this situation proves that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing to release emergency reserves alongside the International Energy Agency, but former IEA official Neil Atkinson notes this will provide only short-term relief; global supply is structurally constrained and will remain low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open sea, through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. Its partial encirclement by Iran's coastline gives Tehran strategic control over transit. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices well above pre-war levels. This discussion connects to [an earlier report on a tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets alike.

The main risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels daily flowing south may now appear insignificant, if this current becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; this, the outlet argues, would be a larger strategic loss than opening the strait.
