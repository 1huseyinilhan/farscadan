Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a subject of debate in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative outlet Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; traffic has slowed significantly and the cost of energy transit has risen. The paper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel—compared to $70 before the conflict—signal that the strait blockade is working to some degree. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly developing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. Though these routes currently carry small volumes, they risk permanently eroding the waterway's geopolitical leverage over time. For this reason, the paper warns that keeping the strait closed long-term could produce more dangerous consequences than reopening it.

The hardline newspaper Kayhan frames things differently. In a report titled "Day 218 of war," it notes that Trump has repeated his claim for seven months that oil prices would fall once the conflict ends—yet prices remain $30 above pre-war levels. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from strategic reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103, Kayhan argues. The paper sees this as proof that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready to release emergency reserves alongside the International Energy Agency, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will provide only short-term relief while global supply remains structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway running from the Persian Gulf to open ocean through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. Its partial enclosure by Iranian coastline gives Tehran strategic control over traffic. During wartime, security risks in the strait and Iran's imposed restrictions have driven oil prices well above pre-conflict levels. This debate connects to an earlier story on [a tanker that was struck](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): every security incident in the strait becomes a fresh calculation for both Tehran's political camps and global energy markets.

The key risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. Though the several million barrels daily flowing around the strait currently seem minor, if this pattern becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years—a bigger strategic loss than reopening it, the paper suggests.
