Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a topic of debate in the Iranian press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the status of the strait is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100—compared to $70 before the war—suggest that the blockade of the strait has worked to some degree. However, the article points out the growing establishment of "workaround" routes: pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these routes currently carry modest volumes of oil, in the long term they could permanently reduce the geopolitical value of the strait. For this reason, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

The hardline Kayhan, by contrast, frames the issue differently. In a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has been repeating "oil prices will drop when the war ends" for seven months, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. It says the market dismisses Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply and that Brent crude has climbed back to $103 despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from its strategic reserve. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the American economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is prepared—together with the International Energy Agency—to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, as global supply would remain structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass; because it is partially surrounded by Iran's coastline, Tehran has strategic control over it. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices well above prewar levels. This debate is linked to [the earlier report](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/) about a tanker being struck: every security incident in the strait becomes a new bargaining point for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets alike.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels of oil diverting south may now seem insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, the deterrent power of the strait could be weakened for years to come—a bigger strategic loss, in the newspaper's view, than opening it.
