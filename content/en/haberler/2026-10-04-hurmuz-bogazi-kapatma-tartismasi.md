Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; the majority of traffic has been disrupted, and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper characterizes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel compared to $70 before the conflict are a sign that the strait closure has worked to some degree. However, the article points out that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly establishing "workaround" routes, including pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait; although these routes currently carry relatively small volumes of oil, they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over the long term. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce a result more dangerous than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the issue differently: in a report titled "Day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the conflict. It states that the market is disregarding Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply; despite the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the American economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing, together with the International Energy Agency, to release emergency reserves, but that according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this would provide only short-term relief, while global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of the world's oil is shipped. Its partial enclosure by Iran's coastline provides Tehran with a strategic leverage point. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices well above prewar levels. This debate is linked to [a previous report on the tanker incident](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security event in the strait becomes a matter of recalculation for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. While a few million barrels of oil currently flowing south of the strait may appear insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—which the newspaper argues would be a greater strategic loss than opening it.
