Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a matter of debate in Iran's press for months. According to a commentary published by moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the status of the strait is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; the majority of traffic is disrupted and energy transit has become more costly. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100, compared to $70 before the war, indicate that the blockade of the strait is working to some degree. However, the article points to the growing entrenchment of "workaround" routes—pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, as well as ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. These routes may carry only small amounts of oil for now, but they could permanently diminish the strait's geopolitical value over time. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could prove more dangerous than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan presents a different frame. In a report titled "day 218 of the war," it notes that Trump has been repeating for seven months that "oil prices will drop when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and the U.S. announcement of 40 million barrels released from reserves, Brent crude climbed back to $103. Kayhan argues this shows that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also cites former IEA official Neil Atkinson as noting that the European Union's readiness to release emergency reserves with the International Energy Agency would provide only short-term relief, while global supply will remain structurally low.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which a significant portion of the world's oil passes, exiting the Persian Gulf to open sea. Iran's position along its shores gives Tehran a strategic control option. In wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and Iran's restrictions have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [the earlier report on the tanker incident](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security event in the strait becomes a recalculation point for both political camps in Tehran and for global energy markets.

The chief risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, while a few million barrels flowing around the strait may seem insignificant now, if that flow becomes permanent it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a larger strategic loss, the newspaper argues, than opening it.
