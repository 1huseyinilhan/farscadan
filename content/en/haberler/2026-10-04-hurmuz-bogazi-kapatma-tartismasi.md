Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a contested question in Iran's press for months. According to an assessment published by moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit costs have risen. The newspaper describes this as an important lever in Iran's hands.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100 per barrel compared with $70 before the war are evidence that the strait blockade has partially worked. However, the article highlights how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq have increasingly established "workaround" routes—pipeline routes and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While these currently carry only modest volumes of oil, over the long term they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value. For this reason, Khabaronline argues, keeping the strait closed indefinitely may produce a more dangerous outcome than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan presents a different frame: In a report titled "War day 218," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than pre-war levels. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises to increase supply and the U.S. announcement of a 40-million-barrel drawdown, Brent crude climbed back to $103. According to Kayhan, this proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready with the International Energy Agency to release emergency reserves, but according to former IEA official Neil Atkinson, this will bring only short-term relief; global supply will remain structurally depressed.

## Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to open ocean and carries a significant portion of the world's oil shipments. Iran's position on both shores gives Tehran strategic control over this passage. In wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions Iran has imposed have driven oil prices substantially above pre-war levels. This debate connects to [earlier reporting on the tanker strike](../2026-10-03-hurmuz-tanker/): each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculation point both for Tehran's political factions and for global energy markets.

The chief risk that Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. Even though the diversions—a few million barrels routing around the strait's southern edge—currently seem minor, if they become permanent, this could weaken the strait's coercive power for years; according to the newspaper, this would be a larger strategic loss than opening the strait.
