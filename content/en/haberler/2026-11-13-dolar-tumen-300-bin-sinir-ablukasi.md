According to market analyst Milad Zamani, citing Ettela'at's report from tecarat-news, a dollar rate of 300,000 tomans "would not be unexpected" given current economic conditions. The report says the dollar rate in recent days is being shaped less by a single economic factor than by the market's stance as it awaits clarity on the country's political and military outlook: sanctions and trade restrictions are not declining but expanding to land and border routes, while news and speculation about a possible deal are pushing some investors to expect a rate correction.

In his assessment to Tejaratnews, Zamani said: "In the current situation, new restrictions have been added to the country's trade routes on top of earlier sanctions. While sea and air sanctions continue after the war, restrictions have now spread to land and border routes as well." The analyst continued: "In recent days, there have been problems at Pakistan and Iraq borders. Even gas tanker exports through the Pakistan route have been halted. In other words, contrary to some expectations, there has been no positive and decisive development in foreign trade; sanctions pressure continues."

Zamani explained the relative stabilization of the rate in its current band as follows: "The only development seen in the foreign-exchange market is that the market has entered a waiting phase. Traders are waiting for the outcome of military and political developments and whether the constantly reported deal will actually materialize." Reminding that agreement rumors have often surfaced in the past but failed to materialize, Zamani said: "Military movement reports of various parties in the region are being published simultaneously with deal news; for this reason, I cannot assess the likelihood of a permanent dollar decline based solely on deal stories." Zamani noted that while some domestic investors are betting on a rate correction given the weight of deal news, he himself does not place great weight on this scenario: "The market is mostly waiting and hoping for clarity on the future."

Zamani stated that a marked decline should not be expected, saying: "Given the constraints and shortages in the economy, the dollar rate in its current band is holding and as year-end approaches, the trading band for this note appears to be gradually tightening." Pointing to seasonal demand, Zamani said: "As we approach late autumn and the start of the solar new year, past patterns suggest increased foreign-exchange demand in early winter; this seasonal demand could also affect the market, so it is not possible to forecast a lower band at this time." Zamani offered this assessment of the dollar outlook: "In this environment of uncertainty—neither peace nor war—the currency market cannot safely shift in a downward direction. So the rate is merely holding in its current band and could again reach the 300,000-toman band." Zamani concluded: "I hope the dollar rate does not climb above the current band; but given the economy's current constraints and trade restrictions, a rise of the dollar rate to the 300,000-toman band would not be unexpected."

In Ettela'at's report, Zamani is identified as a "market expert and economic analyst" whose assessments come from statements to Tejaratnews, an economics news site. According to the report, Zamani's main emphasis is that what now determines the rate's direction is no longer a single economic indicator but the flow of news about the war's course and a possible U.S.-Iran deal. The report notes that Zamani reminded observers that similar "deal is near" rumors in earlier periods created only short-lived market optimism, and most such expectations failed to translate into actual rate movements.

Zamani also said that global gold-per-ounce prices are affected by war news, oil prices, and U.S. employment figures. "When oil prices rise, global gold can come under pressure and lose value," the analyst noted. He said gold-per-ounce prices could target the $4,200 band in coming days and did not expect a significant correction in the short term barring a severe international development; however, if fighting escalates beyond current levels, gold could fall back to $3,700 per ounce. Zamani noted that domestic gold prices are highly correlated with the dollar rate, and that spikes in the dollar rate can at most dampen the rise in domestic gold prices by 3–4 percent.
