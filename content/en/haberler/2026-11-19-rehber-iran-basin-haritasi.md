To understand how a news story is reported in Iran, you first need to know who owns the outlet reporting it. The same official statement may be published in neutral language by IRNA, in triumphalist language by Kayhan, or through an opposition frame by Iran International. This difference stems not from journalistic choice, but from each outlet's affiliation. The source label that Farsçadan adds to every article—"State news agency," "Hardline," "Opposition, diaspora," and so forth—exists to summarize this exact map.

This guide compares the founding history, ownership structure, and political position of eight outlets that Farsçadan consults most regularly: two state agencies, two semi-official agencies, three newspapers, and one opposition channel broadcasting from outside Iran. It draws on independent sources, with every claim attributed to its own source. According to an assessment by the Council on Foreign Relations (CFR), Iran's constitution mandates state control of radio and television, and their leadership is appointed by the Supreme Leader; the written press has no such monopoly. According to CFR, an analyst from Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty's former Farsi service director, Fairbanks, states that virtually every Iranian newspaper is tied to either a political faction or an influential person. The ownership information in this guide rests on official institutional statements, encyclopedic sources, and current evaluations by media monitoring organizations.

## Three layers: state, semi-official, and private/municipal press

According to CFR's assessment, Iranian news organizations can be broadly grouped into three layers. The first consists of official agencies directly tied to a state ministry; IRNA exemplifies this layer most clearly. The second consists of outlets that appear "independent" but which observers believe reflect the government's or security apparatus's agenda; CFR identifies Fars as the voice of judicial circles and ISNA as representing academic circles. The third layer comprises newspapers and news websites; some (like Hamshahri) are tied to a public institution, others (like Donya-ye Eqtesad) to private capital, and others (like Kayhan) directly to the Supreme Leader's office. Outside these three layers stands a fourth: channels broadcasting outside Iran that Tehran labels "opposition" or "hostile" (such as Iran International).

## Who speaks for whom: ownership details

**State agencies.** According to State Media Monitor (SMM), IRNA has fallen into the "state-controlled" category every reporting cycle since 2022; an expert consulted by SMM estimated that roughly 80% of IRNA's operating budget comes directly from state allocation. ISNA's history is more layered: SMM notes the agency was founded in 1999 by Tehran university students during the peak of the reformist movement, and its chain of command runs to a non-state academic institution created in 1980—Jihad-e Daneshgahi—rather than directly to a ministry or the Supreme Leader's office. SMM's 2025 assessment notes that ISNA's editorial space has narrowed and the agency has shifted from "independently state-funded" to "state-controlled" category.

**IRGC-affiliated agencies.** Tasnim and Fars were sanctioned by the U.S. Treasury on September 15, 2023, for acting on behalf of the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps (IRGC); Treasury stated that Tasnim was founded by IRGC commanders, and that Fars's board chair is a senior IRGC Intelligence official. These two agencies frequently publish "exclusive" reports credited to "informed sources," particularly on security and defense matters; newspapers like Kayhan and Ettela'at typically relay military claims by citing these agencies.

**Supreme Leader's line newspaper.** According to Wikipedia, Kayhan was founded in 1943 and became a state-backed outlet after the 1979 Revolution; its editors have since been appointed directly by the Supreme Leader. The newspaper's editor-in-chief, Hussein Shariatmadari, is formally known as "the Supreme Leader's representative."

**Established mainstream daily.** According to Wikipedia, Ettela'at was founded in 1926 by Abbas Massoudi and remains the oldest continuously published Farsi daily newspaper in the world. Post-1979, it became "state-backed" alongside Kayhan and Jomhouri-ye Eslami; yet in Farsçadan's source labels, Ettela'at is classified as "moderate-traditionalist" because of its measured, institutional language in daily reporting—distinct from Kayhan—a reminder that ownership structure and daily editorial line do not always align perfectly.

**Municipal newspaper.** According to Wikipedia, Hamshahri was founded on December 15, 1992, by Tehran's then-mayor Gholamhossein Karbaschi and remains owned by the Tehran Municipality. Its management shifts when the mayor changes, and its editorial line has sometimes swung with the political camp of the sitting mayor.

**Privately owned economics newspaper.** According to Wikipedia, Donya-ye Eqtesad was founded in October 2002 with no formal government ties; it follows a free-market, center-right editorial line. Its owner, Alireza Bakhtiari, also heads the DEN Group, which publishes the English-language Financial Tribune.

**News website linked to Larijani circle.** According to Khabaronline's own masthead, the site began publishing in 2008–2009 (1387 in the Iranian calendar). According to Tehran Bureau, citing statements from government journalists, the person said to have editorial influence over the site is Ali Larijani, who directed state broadcasting from 1994 to 2004 and strengthened his political network during that period; this affiliation rests on source statements rather than formal ownership records.

**Opposition channel abroad.** According to Wikipedia, Iran International launched in May 2017 from London and is owned by Volant Media UK Ltd, registered there. The channel's funding is contested: a 2018 Guardian article cited by Wikipedia suggests backing from figures close to Saudi Arabia's crown prince; the channel denies this and asserts editorial independence. Iran designated the channel a terrorist organization in November 2022.

## Iranian press by the numbers

- According to an SMM expert estimate, roughly 80% of IRNA's operating budget comes from direct state allocation.
- According to Wikipedia's infobox, Hamshahri's daily circulation in 2015 was 540,000 copies; Donya-ye Eqtesad's circulation (by an undated Wikipedia figure) is listed as 50,000 copies.
- By a U.S. Treasury decision dated September 15, 2023, Tasnim and Fars were sanctioned under Executive Order 13553 for acting on behalf of the IRGC.
- According to Wikipedia, in November 2017 Kayhan was banned from publishing for two days by order of Tehran's prosecutor for a headline; this shows that even a hardline newspaper can clash with official policy.
- According to the Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ), Iran International anchor Pouria Zeraati was stabbed in London on March 29, 2024; two people were convicted of the attack in June 2026.

## How it's discussed in the Persian press

Some terms for classifying the Iranian press recur in Farsi sources. **خبرگزاری رسمی** (phonetic: *khabarguza'ri-ye resmi*), "official news agency," refers to agencies like IRNA that report directly to a ministry. **نیمه‌رسمی** (*nime-resmi*), "semi-official," describes outlets like Tasnim, Fars, and ISNA that appear independent but are linked to an institution. **اصولگرا** (*osulgara*, meaning "principlist" or "traditionalist") describes the conservative faction that prioritizes early-revolutionary principles and the Supreme Leader's authority; **اصلاح‌طلب** (*eslah-talab*, "reformist") describes the faction advocating for political opening within the system. The term **رسانه دشمن** (*resane-ye doshan*, "enemy media") is used by officials for foreign outlets like Iran International, and you will encounter this label frequently in Farsçadan's translated articles; for instance, Kayhan's report on today's (November 19) Abadan incident frames protests through this lens, attributing them to "enemy" influence.

## From Turkey's perspective

This map has practical value for readers tracking Iranian news from Turkey: the same event can look very different depending on which outlet covers it. For a reader interested in economics and trade, Donya-ye Eqtesad may serve as a reference source because it has no formal government ties and reports based on market data; the same story in IRNA may reflect the state's official line, while a Kayhan version may echo views from security-aligned circles. A single clip of Iranian news shared on social media (especially X and Telegram) without the outlet's name and orientation can be misleading. Farsçadan's practice of tagging each article with the source name and orientation is designed to prevent precisely this kind of decontextualized sharing.
