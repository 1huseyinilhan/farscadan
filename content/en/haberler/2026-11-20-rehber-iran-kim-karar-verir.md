When a Farsçadan article states something like "the Assembly of Experts called for strict enforcement of hijab" or "the head of the Expediency Council made a statement," most readers pass by without knowing how that institution differs from Parliament, let alone from the Supreme Leader. Yet in Iran, the question of who decides cannot be answered by a single office; the constitution distributes power among six interlocking institutions, some elected, some appointed, and some a hybrid of both.

This guide, based on Iran's 1979 constitution as amended in 1989, compares six core institutions—the Supreme Leader, the President, Parliament, the Guardian Council, the Expediency Discernment Council, and the Assembly of Experts. Sources include the unofficial English translation of the constitution on Constitute Project, a current assessment from the Council on Foreign Relations (CFR), and Wikipedia's relevant institution articles; every claim is attributed to its source.

## Six institutions, one system

According to CFR's assessment, power in Iran is divided between elected bodies (the Presidency, Parliament, and the Assembly of Experts) and unelected, religiously rooted institutions (the Office of the Supreme Leader, the Guardian Council, and the Expediency Council). Yet as CFR underscores, these two layers are not independent: candidates for elected offices are first vetted by an unelected body (the Guardian Council), laws passed by the elected Parliament must pass through the same council again, and according to Article 113 of the constitution, the President governs the state only in areas outside those reserved to the Supreme Leader.

## Supreme Leader and Assembly of Experts: who chooses the office, and who oversees it

According to Article 110 of the constitution, the [Supreme Leader](../../en/sozluk/rehberlik-makami/) holds powers including setting general policy, serving as commander-in-chief of the armed forces, and appointing the head of the judiciary; according to CFR, the Supreme Leader also appoints the top commanders of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and the police. According to Article 107 of the constitution, the **Assembly of Experts**—a body elected by the people—decides who fills this position. Article 111 gives the assembly the duty to elect a new leader "as soon as possible" if the incumbent dies, resigns, or is removed.

According to Wikipedia's article on the Assembly of Experts, the body comprises 88 members elected directly by the people for eight-year terms; the most recent election was held on March 1, 2024. However, candidates are vetted first by the Guardian Council: according to the same Wikipedia source, in the 2016 election, only 166 of 801 applicants were approved. According to Wikipedia's article, since the assembly was founded in 1979, it has never formally removed a sitting Supreme Leader or held one accountable; CFR also notes that the assembly's oversight function is conducted in secret committees, leaving it unclear whether this acts as a real check on Khomeini's or Khamenei's power. The first time the assembly wielded this power after 1979 came in the war's first week, following Ali Khamenei's death; the assembly elected a new Supreme Leader in March 2026.

## President and Parliament: two elected bodies with bounded authority

According to the Constitute Project's constitutional text, Article 114 provides that the President is elected directly by the people for a four-year term and can serve at most two consecutive terms; Article 117 provides for a runoff election between the top two candidates if no candidate wins an outright majority. According to CFR, the President heads the government, implements laws, prepares the budget, and proposes cabinet members, but these proposals require parliamentary approval. According to the same source, the President ultimately answers to the Supreme Leader; Article 122 of the constitution also defines the President's duties as owed to the people, the Supreme Leader, and Parliament. Article 110, paragraph 10, grants the Supreme Leader authority to remove the President either upon a determination of constitutional violation by the Supreme Court or following a vote of no-confidence by Parliament under Article 89.

According to Constitute Project's Article 64, Parliament (officially the Islamic Consultative Assembly) began with 270 seats and can grow by no more than 20 seats per decade; according to CFR's current figures, it has grown to 290 seats, five of which are reserved for religious minorities. Members are elected directly for four-year terms on a district basis, as stated in Articles 62 and 63. According to CFR, while Parliament's legislative authority is broad, the percentage of clergy in Parliament has fallen from over 50 percent in 1980 to 5.5 percent by 2020, showing how the body's composition has shifted over time.

## Guardian Council and Expediency Discernment Council: gatekeeper and arbiter

According to Wikipedia's article on the Guardian Council, this 12-member body includes six members (jurists) appointed directly by the Supreme Leader, whom the Leader can dismiss at will; the other six are elected by Parliament from candidates proposed by the chief justice. According to the same source, members serve six-year terms with half the membership rotating every three years. Articles 94 and 96 of the Constitute Project's constitutional text state that every law passed by Parliament is sent to this council within ten days; a majority of the jurists on the council determine conformity with Islam, while a majority of all council members determine conformity with the constitution. According to Wikipedia, since 1991 the council has also vetted candidates for the Presidency, Parliament, and the Assembly of Experts; in the 2009 presidential election, only four of the 476 applicants were approved.

The **Expediency Discernment Council**, according to Wikipedia's article, was established by a decision Khomeini issued on February 6, 1988, and was formalized by a constitutional amendment a year later (Article 112); its purpose is to resolve disputes between Parliament and the Guardian Council and to provide consultation to the Supreme Leader on general policy. According to the same source, members of the council are appointed by the Supreme Leader for five-year terms; the Wikipedia infobox shows the council currently has 48 seats and has been chaired by Sadegh Larijani since December 2018. Its previous chairs were Ali Khamenei (1988–89), Ali Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani (1989–2017, died in office), and Mahmoud Hashemi Shahrudi (2017–18, also died in office).

## Powers and selection methods by institution

| Institution | Members | Selection method | Term | Primary powers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Supreme Leader | 1 | Elected by Assembly of Experts (Art. 107) | Unlimited (removal possible, Art. 111) | General policy, commander-in-chief of armed forces, appoints judiciary head (Art. 110) |
| President | 1 | Direct popular election (Art. 114) | 4 years, max. 2 consecutive terms | Heads government, implements laws, prepares budget, proposes cabinet (Arts. 113–134) |
| Parliament | 290 (CFR) | Direct popular election by district (Arts. 62–64) | 4 years | Legislation, budget approval, parliamentary questioning of ministers (Arts. 71, 89) |
| Guardian Council | 12 (6+6) | Half appointed by Supreme Leader; half elected by Parliament from chief justice nominees | 6 years, half rotate every 3 years | Approve or veto laws and election candidates (Arts. 91–96) |
| Expediency Discernment Council | 48 (current) | Appointed by Supreme Leader | 5 years | Resolve Parliament–Council disputes, advise Supreme Leader (Art. 112) |
| Assembly of Experts | 88 | Direct popular election (candidates vetted by Guardian Council) | 8 years | Elect, oversee, and if necessary remove the Supreme Leader (Arts. 107–111) |

## Iran's power structure in numbers

- According to CFR, the percentage of clergy in Parliament fell from above 50 percent in 1980 to 5.5 percent by 2020.
- According to Wikipedia, the Guardian Council approved only 4 of the 476 applicants in the 2009 presidential election.
- According to Wikipedia, in the 2016 Assembly of Experts election, 166 of 801 candidates were approved by the Guardian Council.
- According to CFR, the Guardian Council has rejected Mahmoud Ahmadinejad's candidacy in every presidential election since 2017.
- According to Wikipedia, two former heads of the Expediency Discernment Council (Rafsanjani and Shahrudi) died in office.

## How the Persian press refers to these institutions

The Persian press typically uses the full official names of these institutions. **مجلس خبرگان رهبری** (pronounced *Majles-e Khobregan-e Rahbari*) means "Assembly of Experts on Leadership" and is referred to in English as the "Assembly of Experts." **شورای نگهبان** (*Shura-ye Negahban*) means "Guardian Council" and is translated as "Guardian Council." **مجمع تشخیص مصلحت نظام** (*Majma-ye Tashkhis-e Maslahat-e Nizam*) is translated as "Expediency Discernment Council." **مجلس شورای اسلامی** (*Majles-e Shura-ye Eslami*) means "Islamic Consultative Assembly" and is referred to in English articles as "Parliament." A November 13 Farsçadan article reporting the Assembly of Experts' discussion of NPT withdrawal, and an October 18 article on the assembly's statement regarding hijab enforcement, demonstrate that the Assembly of Experts is not confined to selecting the Supreme Leader; it also exercises political weight through public statements.

## From Turkey's perspective

This power map carries a practical implication for a Turkish reader following Iran: Ankara's counterpart varies depending on the subject matter. Diplomatic and commercial negotiations usually take place through the Presidency and Foreign Ministry, but according to CFR, final decisions on matters of war, the nuclear program, and regional alliances rest with the Supreme Leader. Kalibaf's sharp statement as Speaker of Parliament on October 10 directed at U.S. Secretary of State Rubio, or the October 26 article reporting CIA leaks that reportedly named Kalibaf as a target, demonstrate that the Speaker's office is a visible actor not just in legislation but also in foreign policy discourse. For Turkish readers, knowing which Iranian institution is making a statement helps them judge both the statement's binding force and its weight.
