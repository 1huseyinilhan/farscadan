Terms that appear frequently in Farsçadan's coverage—such as "JCPOA," "the IAEA Board in Vienna," "enrichment rate," or "trigger mechanism"—often remain opaque to readers unfamiliar with the legal framework underlying Iran's nuclear program. Yet this framework—the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), the International Atomic Energy Agency's (IAEA) inspection authority, the 2015 Comprehensive Joint Action Plan (JCPOA), and that agreement's "snapback" provision—forms the legal skeleton of the 2025–2026 crisis.

This guide addresses four topics in sequence: the history of Iran's relationship with the NPT and IAEA, the concrete limits set out in the JCPOA and their scheduled expiration dates ("sunset" provisions), how the snapback mechanism worked when activated in 2025, and where the program stands today. Sources include the Arms Control Association's summary of the JCPOA, relevant Wikipedia articles, and current news sources; every date and figure is attributed to its source.

## NPT, IAEA, and the Additional Protocol: foundational concepts

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), according to Wikipedia, opened for signature on July 1, 1968, and entered into force on March 5, 1970. The treaty rests on three pillars: preventing the spread of nuclear weapons, nuclear disarmament, and the right to peaceful use of nuclear technology. According to UN counts, 191 countries are parties; outside this number are India, Pakistan, and Israel, which have never joined, and North Korea, which notified withdrawal on January 10, 2003, with the withdrawal taking effect on April 10, 2003—the only example of a state exiting the treaty. The United States, Russia, China, France, and the United Kingdom are the five "nuclear-weapon states" recognized by the treaty; they also serve as the five permanent members of the UN Security Council.

Iran, by the same source, signed and ratified the NPT in July 1968; the treaty entered into force for Iran on March 5, 1970. Every state party to the NPT must sign a "comprehensive safeguards agreement" that opens its nuclear material to IAEA inspection. Beyond this baseline oversight regime, the IAEA Board approved in May 1997 a model text known as the "Additional Protocol," which grants states the authority to conduct short-notice inspections without prior warning and to demand broader facility declarations. Iran, according to Wikipedia's timeline of Iran's nuclear program, voluntarily signed the Additional Protocol on December 18, 2003, within the framework of the Paris Agreement with France, Germany, and the UK (the E3), and began implementing it; however, it never ratified the protocol through its parliament. In February 2006, Tehran ended the voluntary implementation and resumed enrichment at Natanz; the IAEA Board subsequently referred the Iranian file to the UN Security Council.

## Chronology: 1970 to 2026

- **1957:** According to Wikipedia, the program began under a civil nuclear cooperation agreement signed as part of President Dwight Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" initiative.
- **March 5, 1970:** The NPT entered into force for Iran.
- **August 2002:** Following disclosures by the exiled opposition group National Council of Resistance of Iran (NCRI), construction at Natanz and Arak was confirmed through satellite imagery.
- **December 18, 2003:** Iran signed the Additional Protocol.
- **February 2006:** Voluntary implementation ended, the file went to the UN Security Council; Resolution 1696, adopted in July 2006, called under Chapter VII of the UN Charter for Iran to cease all enrichment activities.
- **November 24, 2013:** A preliminary agreement (Joint Action Plan) was signed in Geneva; Iran agreed to halt enrichment above 5% and not to operate the Arak reactor.
- **July 14, 2015:** JCPOA negotiations concluded in Vienna ("Implementation Day"); the UN Security Council approved the agreement on July 20, 2015, via Resolution 2231.
- **January 16, 2016:** "Adoption Day"—the IAEA certified Iran's compliance, and most sanctions were lifted.
- **May 8, 2018:** The United States, under the Trump administration, withdrew from the agreement unilaterally; the lifted sanctions were gradually reimposed.
- **May 8, 2019:** Iran announced suspension of some JCPOA obligations; the IAEA confirmed in July 2019 that the 300-kilogram stockpile limit and 3.67% enrichment threshold had been breached.
- **February 2021:** The Iranian parliament ordered suspension of Additional Protocol implementation in response to U.S. "maximum pressure" sanctions.
- **April 16, 2021:** Following an explosion at Natanz on April 11, 2021, according to Al Jazeera, Iran moved to 60% enrichment after midnight—the highest level it has achieved.
- **June 2025:** During the period known publicly as the "Twelve Days of War," Israel and the United States struck Iran's nuclear facilities (including Natanz and Fordow); Tehran suspended cooperation with the IAEA in early July, and the agency withdrew its inspectors that month.
- **August 28, 2025:** France, Germany, and the United Kingdom (E3) notified the UN Security Council of snapback.
- **September 28, 2025:** The snapback mechanism was triggered; UN sanctions formally reentered.
- **February–March 2026:** The United States and Israel conducted a second wave of strikes targeting Natanz; in its February 2026 report, the IAEA announced it had no access to any of Iran's four declared enrichment sites.
- **April 2026:** A ceasefire was achieved through Pakistani mediation, and senior-level talks began in Islamabad.


## What the JCPOA provided, and what remains?

According to the Arms Control Association, the JCPOA limited Iran's enrichment to 3.67% uranium-235 for 15 years and capped its total stockpile (in all forms) below 300 kilograms over the same period. The number of operating centrifuges was limited to 5,060 IR-1 models for 10 years (out of 6,104 total installed); more than 13,000 excess centrifuges were removed and stored under IAEA supervision. The Fordow facility was converted to a research center, dedicated to stable isotope production in cooperation with Russia, and barred from uranium feed for 15 years; of 1,044 centrifuges in six cascades, 328 were kept in production and 700 in standby. The core of the heavy-water reactor at Arak was removed and redesigned to reduce weapons-grade plutonium production; reprocessing was prohibited for 15 years, as was construction of new heavy-water reactors.

The agreement's restrictions do not expire on a single date but on a staggered "sunset" timeline:

| Duration | Lifted restriction |
| --- | --- |
| 5 years | UN arms embargo |
| 8 years | Missile-related UN/EU/U.S. restrictions |
| 10 years | Centrifuge production ban, snapback option, UN file closure |
| 15 years | 3.67% enrichment and 300 kg stockpile limit, Fordow/Arak constraints |
| 20 years | Continuous monitoring of centrifuge production facilities |
| 25 years | Monitoring of uranium mines and mills |
| Indefinite | Ban on weaponization activities |

According to this timeline, "Implementation Day" was set for January 16, 2016, "Transition Day" for October 18, 2023, and "Termination Day"—when the UN Security Council would close the file—for October 18, 2025. That same date was also the snapback option's last-use date.

## The snapback mechanism and the 2025–2026 crisis

The snapback is a mechanism created by UN Security Council Resolution 2231 (July 20, 2015), which approved the JCPOA; its purpose is to restore previous UN sanctions without requiring a veto by Russia or China. According to Wikipedia, the mechanism operates when a state party notifies the Security Council that Iran has "significantly failed to perform its commitments"; after this notification, continuation of the sanctions waiver requires a separate Council decision—if no such decision passes or one of the five permanent members vetoes, the previous sanctions resolutions (1696, 1737, 1747, 1803, 1835, 1929, and 2224) automatically reenters.

France, Germany, and the United Kingdom (E3) triggered this process on August 28, 2025; according to the same source, their grounds were Iran's "ongoing and deliberate" failure to meet JCPOA obligations since 2019 and its accumulation of a "non-civil" stockpile of highly enriched uranium. Iran's deputy UN envoy Kazem Gharibabadi had stated on July 25, 2025, that such a step would be "entirely unlawful"; Russia and China also rejected the move, asserting the E3 lacked this authority. Nevertheless, UN sanctions formally reentered on September 28, 2025; in October 2025, Iran, Russia, and China declared the JCPOA expired.


Military developments preceded and followed this diplomatic rupture: In June 2025, Israel and the United States struck Iran's nuclear facilities during the "Twelve Days of War"; Tehran then suspended cooperation with the IAEA and the agency withdrew its inspectors. In February–March 2026, the United States and Israel conducted a second wave of strikes targeting Natanz—developments detailed in Farsçadan's report on the IAEA's Vienna oversight dispute and article on the Experts Assembly's NPT discussion. According to the IAEA's February 2026 report, the agency has had no access to any of Iran's four declared enrichment sites, and it cannot verify whether Iran has suspended all enrichment activity; the agency also does not know the precise location of a fourth facility near Isfahan. The IAEA's last verified figure puts the highly enriched uranium stockpile at approximately 440.9 kilograms as of June 13, 2025; agency director Rafael Grossi said this stockpile could theoretically suffice for about 10 nuclear weapons if weaponized, but emphasized that there is no evidence Iran has taken such a step.

## How the Persian press refers to it

The Persian press refers to the JCPOA through the acronym برجام (Farsi *Barjâm*, derived from the first letters of the full Farsi title "*Barnâme-ye Jâme'-e Eqdâm-e Moştarak*"), which is typically rendered in Turkish coverage as "KOEP." For enrichment, Farsi uses غنی‌سازی (*Ğani-sâzi*). The IAEA appears in Farsi media under its official name, آژانس بین‌المللی انرژی اتمی (*Âjâns-e Beynolmelali-ye Enerji-ye Atomi*), or simply آژانس (*Âjâns*, "Agency"). The Additional Protocol in Farsi is پروتکل الحاقی (*Protokol-e Elhâqi*). For the snapback mechanism, Farsi media commonly uses مکانیسم ماشه (*Mekânism-e Mâşe*)—literally "trigger mechanism"—the word ماشه (*mâşe*, trigger) deriving from the verb "to pull the trigger"; this term appears in Turkish coverage as "trigger mechanism" or by its English equivalent "snapback."

## From Turkey's perspective

The sanctions reimposed by snapback and the regional conflict directly affect Turkey's energy imports. According to Daily Sabah, citing Reuters, Turkey's annual agreement with Iran capping natural gas flow at 9.6 billion cubic meters has frequently delivered below contract volume; according to Energy and Natural Resources Minister Alparslan Bayraktar's April 2026 remarks, Turkey imported 7.6 billion cubic meters from Iran in 2025, representing 13% of Turkey's total gas imports, while regulatory data show the pipeline last reached contract capacity in 2022. Per the same report, the agreement was set to expire in July 2026, and the minister stated at that time that talks on extension had not yet begun "due to current conditions in the region" (this information is from April 2026; the agreement's status after July 2026 could not be verified in this guide's sources). Together with the blockade discussions at Hormuz, each new wave of sanctions on Iran's nuclear file affects Turkey's future under this pipeline agreement.
