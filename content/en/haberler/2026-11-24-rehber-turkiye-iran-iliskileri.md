In Farsçadan's reports, Turkey and Iran can appear as two different faces within a single week: on one hand, agreements to keep shared border crossings open 24 hours; on the other, a soldier killed by fire at the border or migrant smugglers arrested. This duality can seem contradictory to a reader unfamiliar with the relationship's structure. Yet Turkey and Iran are neighbors who share one of the world's longest unchanged borders, and function simultaneously as trading partners and regional competitors.

This guide addresses five subjects in turn: the historical framework of the border and institutional cooperation, targeted versus actual trade volumes, the 1996 natural gas agreement (which expired this year), border crossing points and the wartime surge in migration and smuggling pressure, and finally the regional equation in which the two states are both rivals and partners. Sources include Wikipedia's articles on the Treaty of Zuhab, the Economic Cooperation Organization (ECO), and Iran-Turkey relations; Daily Sabah's reporting on trade and natural gas; PBS/AP coverage of the Kapıköy border crossing; and Farsçadan's own reporting. Each claim is attributed to its source.

## Historical framework: a border spanning 1639 to today

According to Wikipedia's article on the Treaty of Zuhab, the foundation of the Turkey-Iran border was laid by the Treaty of Zuhab, signed May 17, 1639, in Kasr-ı Şirin; this treaty ended the 1623-1639 war between the Ottoman and Safavid empires. Under the treaty, Mesopotamia including Baghdad went to the Ottomans, while East Georgia, East Armenia, Dagestan, and Shirvan went to the Safavids. Per the same source, this line remained the Ottoman-Iran border until 1918 and formed the rough basis of today's Turkey-Iran-Iraq borders. However, the article adds a caveat: because the treaty defined the border as a regional division rather than a precise line, Iran and the Ottomans signed at least 18 separate treaties concerning the border between 1555 and 1918; a Cambridge-published study (Sabri Ateş, 2019) questions how definitively the treaty actually fixed it, bearing the title "Treaty of Zuhab, 1639: Founding Myth or Founding Document?"

In the twentieth century, the two countries formalized this historical legacy. Per Wikipedia's Iran-Turkey relations article, a 1926 Friendship Treaty, a more precise 1932 border treaty, and a 1937 regional cooperation agreement (Saadabad Pact) were signed; the CENTO (Central Treaty Organization) founded in 1955 included both countries. According to Wikipedia's Economic Cooperation Organization (ECO) article, in 1964 Turkey, Iran, and Pakistan created the Regional Cooperation for Development (RCD) for development purposes; this organization lost effectiveness after 1979. The same three countries revived cooperation by founding the ECO in Tahran in 1985. When seven additional former Soviet and neighboring states joined in 1992, the organization's membership rose to today's 10; the ECO aims to establish a "single market for goods and services" among members.

Per the introduction of Wikipedia's Iran-Turkey relations article, this long partnership carries a contradictory character: the two states have long been on opposite sides in regional conflicts such as those in Syria, Libya, and the South Caucasus, while sharing common interests on some issues like Kurdish separatism. This two-way relationship appears in concrete form across the four headings below.

## Trade: ambitious targets, infrastructure steps

Turkey and Iran have repeated the same figure for years: a $30 billion annual bilateral trade volume. According to Daily Sabah, Turkish Trade Minister Ömer Bolat reiterated this goal at the 29th Turkey-Iran Joint Economic Commission meeting held in Tehran on December 11, 2024, saying "we have an opportunity to reach a $30 billion trade volume with determined steps." Per the same report, concrete steps toward this goal included opening two new border crossings with Iran, modernizing existing customs gates, renewing the energy agreement, reviving the 2015 preferential trade agreement, and concluding a comprehensive tourism accord.

Infrastructure steps continued in 2026. According to a Farsçadan report dated November 4, 2026, Iran's Economy Minister and Bolat met in Istanbul on the sidelines of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation's COMCEC meeting and decided to keep the two countries' shared border gates open 24 hours without interruption; the aim was to increase trade volume, reduce truck wait times, and speed up commercial processes. The parties also agreed to establish a working group for joint free-trade-industry zones and to prepare a cargo transit schedule. The Turkey-Iran land border consists of three official customs crossings:

| Border crossing | Province (Turkey) | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gürbulak ↔ Bazergan | Ağrı | Main trade and truck transit; covered by 24-hour operating decision |
| Kapıköy | Van | Passenger transit; became a critical exit during the war |
| Esendere | Hakkari | Mountainous terrain; high smuggling and illegal crossing pressure |

The goal itself is not new, but a single verified figure for actual trade volume appears nowhere in this guide's sources; accordingly, we report only the goal and concrete steps toward it here, with current total trade volume marked as unverified.

## Natural gas: the 1996 agreement and an uncertain future

Turkey-Iran natural gas trade rests on a 25-year agreement signed in 1996. Per a Daily Sabah report from 2016, under this agreement Turkey was importing roughly 10 billion cubic meters annually and Iran was Turkey's second-largest supplier after Russia. Price disagreements prompted Turkey to pursue international arbitration in 2012: Ankara sought a 25% price cut, while Tehran said it would only accept a reduction if Turkey increased import volumes; Turkey declined this condition. In February 2016, the arbitration court ruled in Turkey's favor; Iran's Deputy Oil Minister Hamidreza Araki described the reduction as 10-15%, while Turkey's Energy Minister Berat Albayrak put it at 13.3-15.8% and stated Iran would reimburse amounts overpaid during 2011-2015; Araki estimated compensation at roughly $1 billion. The same report also notes that in a separate 2014 arbitration case, Turkey won a 16.5% discount and $900 million in compensation via BOTAŞ.

The agreement's current status becomes clear through information cited in Farsçadan's nuclear program guide, drawn from Daily Sabah/Reuters sources: according to Energy Minister Alparslan Bayraktar's April 2026 statement, Turkey's actual flows under this gas agreement—with an annual ceiling of 9.6 billion cubic meters—have frequently fallen short of contracted volumes; in 2025, Turkey imported 7.6 billion cubic meters from Iran, equal to 13% of the country's total gas imports. Per the same statement, the 25-year agreement was set to expire in July 2026, and the minister said at that time that renegotiation had not yet begun. As of November 24, 2026—four months after the stated expiration—whether the contract has been renewed remains unverified; this remains an open question requiring monitoring.

## Border: crossings, wartime migration, and smuggling

When the United States and Israel launched a war against Iran in February 2026, the border's day-to-day operations shifted. With Iran's airspace closed, the Kapıköy crossing in Van province became, per a PBS/AP report from March 8, 2026, "one of the most important ways" linking Iranians to the rest of the world. Per the report, most crossing travelers were people with business, family, or friendship ties in Turkey or residence rights in a third country; a hotel operator in Van said most arrivals were "dual nationals who stayed one or two days before flying elsewhere." Turkish Interior Ministry figures showed that on a given Wednesday, 2,032 people entered Turkey from Iran and 1,966 returned to Iran—a reversible, bilateral flow more than a one-way refugee wave; AP reported that only a small fraction of those interviewed said they planned to stay indefinitely.

That same border witnessed fatal events. According to a Farsçadan report citing Hamshahri on November 5, 2026, in the Esendere border outpost area in Hakkari's Yüksekova district, Private Yusuf Sait Ünlü, responding to an illegal border crossing, was killed on October 31 by what Turkish assessments attributed to fire from Iran; Turkey's Defense Ministry announced it launched a coordinated investigation with Iranian authorities and held a joint site visit on November 3. The source of the fire and the circumstances remain independently unverified. On Iran's border side, smuggling pressure continues: per a Farsçadan report citing IRNA on November 15, 2026, in Salmas district on the Turkey border in West Azerbaijan, border guards in three separate operations arrested 41 foreign nationals and 2 smugglers facilitating their illegal departure; the same report notes that Rudaw reported Turkey arrested 132 suspected smuggling organizers in coordinated operations across 24 provinces in April 2026. Thus, this ~500-kilometer border embodies the duality mentioned at this guide's start: on one side, 24-hour-open commercial gates; on the other, fatal incidents and smuggling operations.

## Regional rivalry and cooperation

The contradiction-partnership balance noted in Wikipedia's Iran-Turkey relations article appears also in current 2026 reporting. Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan signed the "Mecca Collective Defense Treaty" in August 2026, stipulating that an attack on any one would be treated as an attack on all three. Yet per a Farsçadan report citing Donya-ye Eghtesad on October 27, 2026, senior officials of these three states (including Turkish National Intelligence Organization Chief Ibrahim Kalın) traveled to Tehran on October 26 to hold a four-way meeting with Iran's Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsin Rezaei; the agenda was reducing regional tensions. Iran's official voice characterized this as a "consensus" reached by the four on continuing the process, but Saudi Arabia's separate statement did not include this language; the meeting's concrete outcome therefore remains unverified. The MIT Chief's participation, however, can be read as an indicator that the defense pact's members still deem direct contact with Iran necessary.

The Kurdish question is one area where the two countries hold common interests. Iran's own Kurdish population makes Turkish developments on the Kurdish issue a subject tracked in Iran's press; per a Farsçadan report from November 3, 2026, the reformist-leaning Khabaronline addressed PKK founder Abdullah Öcalan's life and the organization's 2025 arms-laying decision in a detailed portrait—an example of the contradiction-partnership balance noted by Wikipedia.

## How the Persian press refers to it

In Persian-language press, Turkey appears as **ترکیه** (*Torkiye*). The term for border crossing is **گذرگاه مرزی** (*Gozargâh-e Marzi*); customs is **گمرک** (*Gomrok*). The Persian word for smuggler is **قاچاقچی** (*Qâçâqçi*)—a term also borrowed into Turkish in the same form. The unit responsible for border security appears in Iran's press as **هنگ مرزبانی** (*Hang-e Marzbâni*, Border Guard Corps), which falls under the Interior Ministry. The Mecca Pact appears in Persian outlets as **پیمان مکه** (*Peymân-e Mekke*).

## Turkey's perspective

Each of the four headings in this guide directly concerns Turkey. The fatal incident at the border shows that security risk remains live even as trade diplomacy proceeds. On energy, given that 13% of Turkey's total gas imports come from Iran (per 2025 figures) and the 1996 agreement expired in July 2026, the agreement's current status is an open question Turkey must monitor for energy security. On trade, the 24-hour border gates and COMCEC-level meetings are part of Turkey's effort to position its own land route as an alternative to the Hormuz Strait bottleneck; Foreign Minister Fidan's November 1, 2026 remarks on the damage Hormuz closure inflicts on the global economy occurred in this same timeframe. On migration and smuggling, Turkey managed a largely temporary and bilateral border flow during wartime while continuing routine security measures against irregular migration in Van province.
