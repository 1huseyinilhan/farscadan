According to Donya-e Eqtesad, citing U.S. media, a cyber breach was detected in a network operated by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI); IRNA and ISNA also reported the incident as a "cyberattack on U.S. intelligence agencies." U.S. officials stated that the breach did not affect classified systems.

According to Donya-e Eqtesad, citing The New York Times and Associated Press, the CIA and ODNI detected a breach in a network used to collect and process open-source intelligence (OSINT)—data from social media, commercial satellite imagery, and proprietary databases. According to the report, the full scope of the breach is not yet known, but two sources who spoke to The New York Times suggested that information from other U.S. government agencies may also have been exposed. An official who spoke to the Associated Press said the attack did not involve any classified information and that the problem has been resolved. Donya reported that ODNI stated in its response that there was "no disruption to critical capabilities or classified systems"; the CIA made no direct statement on the matter. IRNA reported the same incident using the phrase "terrorist CIA was hacked," which represents the source's own assessment and has not been independently corroborated in other sources. At the time this story was prepared, the original New York Times/Associated Press report could not be directly accessed; however, a November 19, 2026 Saudi Gazette article reported a separate technical outage at the National Security Agency (NSA) lasting two days, and U.S. National Intelligence Director Jay Clayton told the House that he had "very high confidence" this outage did not result from a cyberattack. The relationship between the alleged CIA/ODNI cyberattack and this official statement that the NSA outage was not from cyberattack remains unverified as of this story's preparation; whether the two incidents are connected remains unclear. According to Donya-e Eqtesad, the alleged breach coincides with a series of technical failures and security incidents the U.S. has experienced recently in government agencies and the private sector: earlier reports had surfaced about a breach of a nationwide database operated by the FBI, containing arrest warrants and background-check queries used by law enforcement; the Department of Agriculture, the Pentagon, and the Department of Homeland Security were also reported to have faced similar cyber incidents. The scope of each of these earlier incidents and their connection to today's CIA/ODNI claim could not be independently verified as of this story's preparation.

## Context

**Background:**
- **July 2025:** The Washington Times reported that a website affiliated with a CIA-linked open-source intelligence program called "Digital Hammer" had been hacked; whether the breach involved classified information remained unclear at the time.
- **November 19, 2026:** According to Saudi Gazette, the NSA experienced a two-day technical outage; U.S. National Intelligence Director Jay Clayton said he had "very high confidence" this was not a cyberattack.
- **November 24-25, 2026:** Based on U.S. media reports, a new breach allegation emerged at a CIA/ODNI open-source intelligence network.

**Who and what:** The Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) is the central hub coordinating 18 U.S. intelligence agencies, established in 2005 as part of post-9/11 intelligence reform. The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) collects and analyzes foreign intelligence and reports directly to the White House, not Congress. "Open-source intelligence" (OSINT) is intelligence derived from public, non-classified data—social media, commercial satellite imagery, news reports—which the allegedly targeted network was designed to process.

**By the numbers:** According to Donya-e Eqtesad, citing U.S. media, within the same week the CIA/ODNI network breach was alleged, similar cyber-security incidents were reported at the FBI, Department of Agriculture, Pentagon, and Department of Homeland Security—at least four different federal agencies. This count is based on media reporting cited in the story, not official cybersecurity reports, and could not be independently verified.

**Next:** ODNI and CIA have not yet released an official, detailed statement on the scope of the breach; the Associated Press reported the problem has been "resolved," but no new assessment timeline has been shared.
