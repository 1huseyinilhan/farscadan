Nearly every week, Farsçadan publishes a story about the Strait of Hormuz. All these reports share one thread: a narrow waterway but of enormous consequence in energy commerce—the Strait of Hormuz. This passage connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and from there to the open ocean, and is the only exit to sea for oil and gas producers in the gulf.

This guide explains the strait's geography, oil and LNG volumes passing through it, major crises from the 1980s to today, and its relationship to Turkey's energy security. For a brief definition and current wartime status, see the [Strait of Hormuz glossary entry](../../sozluk/hurmuz-bogazi/); this guide deepens the numerical and historical context.

## Geography: the world's narrowest energy gateway

According to Wikipedia, the Strait of Hormuz is approximately 167 kilometers long, with the Iranian coast on its north side and Oman's Musandam Peninsula (partly administered by the UAE) on its south, connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman. Its width varies from 97 kilometers to 39 kilometers (21 nautical miles) at its narrowest. Shipping traffic flows in two lanes, each 2 nautical miles wide—one inbound, one outbound—separated by a "midline" buffer. Wikipedia notes that because waters near the UAE coast are relatively shallow, large tankers must use Iranian territorial waters when transiting.

The strait's legal status has been contested. Wikipedia reports that in 1959, Iran extended its territorial waters to 12 nautical miles and declared it would permit only "innocent passage" within this zone. Iran signed UNCLOS but never ratified it; nevertheless, most countries accept that "transit passage" as defined in UNCLOS applies in the strait. This legal gap means every military tension reignites the question of "whose rules apply."

## By the numbers: Hormuz oil and LNG flows

According to the EIA's March 2026 update on "World Oil Transit Chokepoints":

| Period | Oil (million barrels/day) | LNG (billion cubic feet/day) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2020 | 19.2 | 10.7 |
| 2021 | 19.7 | 10.8 |
| 2022 | 21.9 | 11.0 |
| 2023 | 21.8 | 10.6 |
| 2024 | 20.7 | 10.5 |
| 2025 (H1) | 20.9 | 11.4 |

The 20.9 million barrels daily represents approximately 20 percent of global oil consumption and one-quarter of all oil transported by sea. The EIA notes 89 percent flows to Asian markets; China, India, Japan, and South Korea collectively account for 74 percent.

On LNG, the 11.4 billion cubic feet per day represents more than one-fifth of global LNG trade, mostly from Qatar. China is the largest buyer.

Alternative routes are limited. Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline and the UAE's Abu Dhabi pipeline offer theoretical bypass capacity of 4.7 million barrels daily, but realistically usable capacity is about 2.6 million barrels daily. Iran's Goreh-Jask pipeline is limited to approximately 0.3 million barrels daily.

## Historical crises: 1980s through 2019

According to Wikipedia:

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 1981–1988 | "Tanker War" during Iran-Iraq War: Iraq attacked merchant shipping beginning May 1981; after Iraq's 1984 Kharg Island attack, Iran targeted tankers serving Kuwait and Gulf Arab states. |
| April 18, 1988 | U.S. conducted "Operation Praying Mantis," striking Iranian targets in response to U.S. warship damage from mines Iran laid. |
| July 3, 1988 | USS Vincennes mistook an Iran Air A300 for a fighter jet and shot it down over the strait; 290 people were killed. |
| June 13, 2019 | Explosions on tankers *Front Altair* and *Kokuka Courageous*; U.S. blamed Iran, Iran denied claiming "false flag." |
| July 2019 | Iran seized British tanker *Stena Impero*, citing retaliation for *Grace 1* seizure off Gibraltar. |

Wikipedia reports that Iran has also raised closing the strait at other times—2008, 2011–2012, 2018—but prior to the 2026 U.S.-Iran war, the strait was never closed for extended periods. Current wartime status is outside this guide's scope.

## How the Persian press refers to it

The Persian press calls this waterway **تنگه هرمز** (*tenge-ye Hormoz*); Turkish is "Hürmüz Boğazı." For detailed definitions, see the [Strait of Hormuz glossary entry](../../sozluk/hurmuz-bogazi/); for the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps, see the [Sepah glossary entry](../../sozluk/devrim-muhafizlari/).

## Turkey's perspective

According to EIA's Turkey country note, Turkey imports nearly all its oil and liquid fuel; in 2022, domestic production met less than 9 percent of demand. For natural gas, Turkey's main suppliers in 2022 were Russia (39%), Iran (17%), Azerbaijan (16%), and the U.S. (10%, as LNG). BOTAŞ data show Turkey's annual natural gas imports between 2021–2025 ranged from 46 to 59 billion cubic meters.

However, sources examined do not indicate Turkey's oil or gas passes by sea through the Strait of Hormuz. According to the EIA's chokepoint analysis, cargoes to Marmara LNG terminal are "largely" from the U.S. and Algeria; Qatar's LNG through Hormuz serves China, India, and South Korea. Pipeline gas from Iran (Tabriz-Dogubayazit line, since 2001) travels overland, not through the strait. Turkey's direct physical dependence on Hormuz is limited.

Turkey's connection operates through the price channel: each tension surge in the strait reflects in global oil prices, affecting Turkey as an importing nation. When [oil reached $117 per barrel](../2026-10-24-petrol-fiyati-117-dolar-hurmuz-gerginligi/), it affected Turkey's economy. Statements by [Foreign Minister Fidan](../2026-11-01-fidan-hurmuz-bogazi-kuresel-ekonomi/) and [EU Commission President von der Leyen](../2026-11-04-vonderleyen-hurmuz-avrupa-maliyet/) about the strait's closure costs illustrate this indirect effect. [News about Turkey's central bank reserve erosion](../2026-11-24-turkiye-rezerv-iran-savasi/) reflects this indirect economic impact.

Turkey's primary energy corridors lie largely outside Hormuz. The Kirkuk-Ceyhan Pipeline, Baku-Tbilisi-Ceyhan, TANAP, TurkStream, and Blue Stream undersea pipeline are Turkey's principal energy arteries; all operate overland or through the Black Sea/Caucasus and do not pass through Hormuz.
