What does closing the Strait of Hormuz gain Iran? Contradictory accounts in Tehran press

Tehran newspapers debate whether closing the Strait of Hormuz would benefit or harm Iran long-term; crude oil reached $103.

By AI Agent · Editorial oversight: Farsçadan Editors · October 4, 2026 · Source: Khabaronline

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed has been a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an analysis published by the moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the state of the strait is "neither fully open nor fully closed"; traffic has been severely disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper frames this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, crude oil exceeding $100 per barrel, compared to $70 before the conflict, is evidence that the strait's blockade has worked to some degree. However, the article points out how Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq are increasingly establishing "workaround" routes through pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait: although these routes currently carry limited amounts of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's geopolitical value in the long run. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for an extended period could produce more dangerous results than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan paints a different picture: in a report titled "day 218 of war," it notes that Trump has been repeating for seven months that "oil prices will fall when the war ends," yet prices remain $30 higher than before the conflict. Despite Treasury Secretary Bessent's promises of increased supply and the U.S. releasing 40 million barrels from its strategic reserve, it notes that Brent crude rose back to $103. According to Kayhan, this demonstrates that Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union is preparing to release emergency oil reserves in coordination with the International Energy Agency, but according to Neil Atkinson, a former IEA official cited in the article, this will provide only short-term relief; global supply will continue to remain structurally depressed.

Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean through which a significant portion of the world's oil shipments pass; its partial encirclement by Iran's coastlines gives Tehran a strategic control opportunity. Under wartime conditions, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have driven crude prices markedly above pre-war levels. This debate is connected to a previous report on a tanker strike: every security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both Tehran's political factions and global energy markets.

The main risk that Khabaronline highlights is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the article, even if a few million barrels detouring south currently seems insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years; the newspaper frames this as a larger strategic loss than opening the strait.
