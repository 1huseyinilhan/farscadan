What does closing the Strait of Hormuz gain for Iran? Conflicting calculations in Tehran press

Tehran newspapers debate whether closing the Strait of Hormuz benefits Iran long-term; oil has risen to $103.

By AI Agent · Editorial oversight: Farsçadan Editors · October 4, 2026 · Source: Khabaronline

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Whether the Strait of Hormuz is fully open or fully closed remains a question debated in Iran's press for months. According to an assessment published by moderate-conservative Khabaronline, the strait's status is "neither completely open nor completely closed"; most traffic has been disrupted and energy transit has become more expensive. The newspaper defines this as an important lever in Iran's hand.

According to Khabaronline's analysis, oil prices exceeding $100, compared to $70 before the war, suggest the strait closure has been partially effective. Yet the article points to workarounds—pipelines from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq, along with ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait—that are becoming increasingly established. While these routes currently carry small amounts of oil, they could permanently reduce the strait's long-term geostrategic value. For this reason, the newspaper argues, keeping the strait closed for a long time may produce more dangerous consequences than opening it.

Hardline Kayhan frames the issue differently. In a report titled "day 218 of war," it notes that Trump has repeated for seven months that oil prices will fall once the war ends, yet prices remain $30 higher than before the war. Against Treasury Secretary Bessent's pledges to increase supply, markets have ignored these statements, and despite U.S. announcements of releasing 40 million barrels of reserves, Brent crude rose again to $103. Kayhan argues this proves Iran's pressure on the strait is straining the U.S. economy.

Kayhan also reports that the European Union stands ready, alongside the International Energy Agency, to release emergency reserves. However, according to Neil Atkinson, a former IEA official, this would provide only short-term relief, and global supply would remain structurally low.

Context

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to open ocean and handles a significant portion of world oil shipments. Its partial enclosure by Iranian coasts gives Tehran strategic control. During wartime, security risks in the strait and restrictions imposed by Iran have pushed oil prices well above pre-war levels. This debate connects to an earlier report on a tanker attack: each security incident in the strait becomes a new calculus for both political camps in Tehran and global energy markets.

The principal risk Khabaronline emphasizes is the "normalization" of alternative routes. According to the newspaper, while a few million barrels rerouted south currently seems insignificant, if this flow becomes permanent, it could weaken the strait's deterrent power for years to come—a larger strategic loss than opening the strait, in the newspaper's view.
