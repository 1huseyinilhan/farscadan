What is the Revolutionary Guard (Sepah): structure, Basij and Quds Force, difference from Artesh

Structure, branches, Basij and Quds Force relationship, and differences from the regular army, with sourced figures.

By Farsçadan Editors

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Farsçadan's news coverage frequently mentions "Sepah announced," "sources close to the Revolutionary Guards," or "Quds Force"; sometimes the same incident prompts a separate statement from "Artesh" (the regular army). For readers unfamiliar with these two institutions, Iran's security news often becomes a matter of figuring out who is who.

This guide examines the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps' (Sepah, or IRGC in English acronym) founding, its ground-sea-air-space branches, its relationship with Basij and Quds Force, its differences from the regular army (Artesh), and its economic role. Sources include a current Council on Foreign Relations (CFR) assessment, relevant Wikipedia institutional articles, and official U.S. Treasury Department statements; all figures and dates are attributed to their sources.

From 1979 to today: Sepah's founding

According to Wikipedia's IRGC article, the organization was established on May 5, 1979, by Abbas Duzduzani under a decree from Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini days after his return to Tehran on February 1, 1979. According to CFR, Sepah was designed immediately after the overthrow of Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi as a counterbalance to the regular army (Artesh), which the clerical establishment did not trust; Khomeini justified the decision by citing blame placed on the military in a 1953 reading of the Mossadegh coup and described the organization as a "people's army." According to CFR, Sepah initially operated beyond the boundaries of law and judiciary.

Basij, according to Wikipedia, was announced by Khomeini in a statement dated November 26, 1979, and formally established on April 30, 1980; membership initially remained open to citizens aged 18-45. According to the same source, Basij was subordinated to Sepah in 1981, and in October 2009, following the removal of commander Hossein Taeb, it was formally integrated into Sepah's Ground Forces as an internal security measure. Quds Force, by Wikipedia's account, acquired independent unit status in 1988 as a consequence of Sepah's reorganization following the Iran-Iraq War, evolving from the wartime "900th Division," a special intelligence unit. CFR notes that after the 1980-88 Iraq War, Sepah acquired "a command structure resembling Western armies."

Sepah's branches: ground, naval, air-space, Quds Force, Basij

According to CFR, citing data from the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS, 2020), Sepah's total strength exceeds 190,000; roughly half consists of conscripts performing military service. Ground Forces, according to CFR, make up Sepah's largest branch, with more than 150,000 troops stationed in 31 Iranian provinces and Tehran. The Navy has approximately 20,000 personnel; according to Wikipedia's IRGC article, this branch now functions as a de facto coast guard on the Persian Gulf, and its fleet of small fast-attack boats represents a "guerrilla force at sea" approach distinct from regular naval doctrine. Air-Space Forces, according to CFR, number 15,000 personnel and oversee Iran's ballistic missile program.

Quds Force, by CFR's definition, is Sepah's "de facto foreign relations branch." Command passed from Qasim Soleimani—who led from 1998 until his death on January 3, 2020, in a U.S. air strike near Baghdad airport—to his deputy Ismail Qaani the same day. According to CFR, the unit provides training, weapons, money, and military advisory services to Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, the Houthis (Ansarallah) in Yemen, and armed groups in Iraq, Syria, and Afghanistan; the Fatemiyoun Division, composed of Afghan migrants in Syria, is part of this network. Wikipedia's compiled estimates for the unit's size vary widely, from 2,000 to 50,000; the variation reflects different methodologies and years used by different sources.

Figures on Basij's size also vary considerably depending on source: according to Wikipedia's compilation, Iranian official sources cite 20-25 million volunteers, while a 2015 statement to Fars News Agency put the figure at 23.8 million; the U.S. Congressional Research Service (CRS), however, estimates the actively serving contingent at approximately 100,000. Researcher Saeid Golkar told ABC News in a 2022 assessment that the actual active membership is roughly 1 million. CFR reports that according to Basij's own claim, it can mobilize 600,000 volunteers, though this figure represents the organization's own assertion. According to Wikipedia, Basij's duties encompass domestic security, enforcement of moral codes, organization of pro-government demonstrations, and social services.

The difference from Artesh: two parallel armies

According to Wikipedia's Artesh article, the regular army was established in 1921 during the Pahlavi era and continues in its current form after the 1979 Revolution; according to IISS data, it totals 630,000 personnel — 550,000 Ground Forces, 35,000 Air Force, 25,000 Navy, 20,000 Air Defense — and maintains a 2024 budget of $6 billion. According to the same source, Article 143 of the Constitution charges Artesh with "defending the country's independence and territorial integrity," while Article 150 assigns Sepah the duty to "protect the revolution and its achievements"; Artesh faces greater civilian oversight and ideological control than Sepah.

According to Wikipedia, though the two institutions "regularly coordinate," there is "fierce competition" between them; this rivalry stems from unequal access to resources, different levels of influence within the regime, and overlapping duties. According to the same source, the smaller Sepah receives a larger share of the budget; this disparity has at times caused supply shortages in regular forces. Wikipedia also cites some 2026-era claims: according to these unconfirmed reports, some Artesh units have faced shortages of ammunition, food, drinking water, and medical support, tensions that have heightened friction between the two institutions — these claims were not independently verified by Farsçadan.

Political and economic weight

According to CFR, Sepah's command chain runs directly to the Supreme Leader and bypasses the president; the president has no direct control over any armed force (see the guide on who decides in Iran for the broader institutional map). According to CFR, a 2020 assessment by the think tank CSIS stated that Sepah had become "the most powerful controller of all major Iranian economic sectors"; the organization reached this position through reconstruction of infrastructure damaged in the Iran-Iraq War and today maintains extensive holdings in banking, shipping, manufacturing, consumer-goods imports, oil, and infrastructure. According to CFR, political influence translates into no-bid state contracts for Sepah-linked companies; revenues flow to weapons procurement, covert operations, and nuclear program financing. According to the same source, sanctions pushed the organization toward illicit oil sales to China; the U.S. Treasury Department has stated that proceeds from this trade have generated millions for Quds Force and Hezbollah.

The U.S. Treasury Department listed Sepah on October 13, 2017, under counterterrorism authority by way of Presidential Executive Order 13224; the justification was support for Quds Force, which had been on the same list since 2007. According to CFR, the European Union also became the last intergovernmental body to designate Sepah a terrorist organization in 2026, citing its role in suppressing protests. Iran rejects these designations and describes Sepah as a constitutional institution.

How it appears in the Persian press

The Persian press refers to the organization by its full name, سپاه پاسداران انقلاب اسلامی (Farsi pronunciation: Sepâh-e Pâsdârân-e Enqelâb-e Eslâmi), but daily news typically uses the shorthand Sepah. Quds Force appears in the Persian press as نیروی قدس (Niru-ye Qods); in English-language Farsçadan coverage it is rendered as Quds Force. Basij is بسیج in Farsi and means "mobilization"; Farsçadan's reports use the term Basij directly. The regular army is referred to in the Persian press as ارتش (Artesh) and this name carries over directly into English coverage.

In Farsçadan's source tags, the phrase "close to the Revolutionary Guards" refers to news agencies such as Tasnim and Fars, which have widely documented institutional or editorial ties to the organization; the image for this article comes from such an agency (Tasnim) and is a CC BY 4.0 licensed photograph uploaded to Wikimedia Commons.

From Turkey's perspective

Sepah's Naval Forces' operational weight in the Strait of Hormuz and Persian Gulf explains why statements from this branch often predominate in tanker and mine-related news; the operational impact of Hormuz tensions on Turkey's energy import costs was detailed in Farsçadan's report on Araghchi's seven-point statement. In a sanctions wave announced by the U.S. Treasury on November 21, 2026, the son of the CEO of Mahan Air—alleged to be used by Quds Force for weapons and personnel transport—was added to the list; this represented a continuation of the Treasury's September 2026 sanctions wave, which had targeted Turkey-based companies for support to Mahan Air (Farsçadan report). The fact that a defendant in the Fairford case who said "we are Qasim Soleimani's army" in New York was arrested in an Istanbul hotel earlier this year demonstrates how Sepah-linked networks can extend investigation reach into Turkey.

Key terms

  • Revolutionary Guards (Sepah, IRGC)The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, a military, political and economic force founded after the 1979 revolution that answers directly to the Supreme Leader.
  • Quds ForceThe IRGC unit responsible for operations abroad and for relations with allied armed groups in the region.
  • Ansarallah (Houthis)A Zaydi Shia political and military movement from northern Yemen, backed by Iran, that has controlled the capital Sanaa since 2014.
  • Fatemiyoun DivisionAn armed unit the IRGC formed from Afghan migrants living in Iran and deployed in the Syrian war.
  • Office of the Supreme Leader (Velayat-e Faqih)The highest office of state under Iran's constitution. The Supreme Leader, commander-in-chief of the armed forces, is chosen by the Assembly of Experts.
