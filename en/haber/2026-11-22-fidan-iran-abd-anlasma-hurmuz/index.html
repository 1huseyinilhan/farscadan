Fidan: Iran-U.S. deal more important than opening Hormuz

Foreign Minister Fidan said an Iran-U.S. agreement matters beyond reopening Hormuz; war's financial burden strains nations.

By Farsçadan Editors

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan, according to Khabaronline's report, stated Saturday evening in an interview with TGRT Haber that a potential agreement between Iran and the U.S. matters not only for reopening the Strait of Hormuz but also for preventing a global financial crisis. Fidan said he had met with Qatar's Prime Minister that day and that Ankara was doing its part to advance the mediation process.

According to Fidan's reported remarks, Hormuz is a strategic global oil transport route between Iran and Oman, with ongoing message traffic between the parties; both sides have placed their demands on the table. The minister stated that the financial burden of unresolved conflict and crisis weighs heavily on many nations, and he has noticed in discussions with European counterparts (ministers, prime ministers, presidents) that they face severe economic pressures rarely spoken of publicly. Fidan also noted that Turkey's expected steps on CAATSA sanctions, the F-35 program, and S-400 disagreement with the U.S. show that "delays within the Trump administration's authority are now being resolved"; however, he added that the issue has become "a competition between America First and Israel First lobbies" over the past eighteen months.

The minister called the Xi-Putin-Trump trilateral meeting at the APEC summit in China "significant," saying the post-WWII hegemonic order no longer functions and the U.S. is reassessing its global strategy under Trump; he did not expect the summit to produce "a bold and decisive step" but called direct dialogue between nuclear powers "extraordinarily important." Fidan said Turkey's "Mecca Accord" alliance—which he founded—is progressing well in institutionalization, with secretariat and military headquarters details nearly finalized, that the alliance targets no country and guarantees members will not attack each other, and that criticism likening it to NATO Article 5 represents "political exaggeration" in language.

Context

Background:

  • July 2019: U.S. removed Turkey from F-35 joint production program over Russia S-400 purchase.
  • December 2020: Washington imposed CAATSA sanctions on Turkey Defense Industries Presidency.
  • June 2026: Iran and U.S. agreed on "Islamabad Accord Memorandum" through Qatar mediation.
  • November 21, 2026: Araghchi reiterated 7 conditions for opening Hormuz (see Araghchi: 7 conditions for Hormuz).

Who/concept: Hakan Fidan is Turkey's Foreign Minister; previously served as MIT (National Intelligence Organization) Director. CAATSA (Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act) is a 2017 U.S. law imposing sanctions on countries pursuing defense/energy ties with Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

By the numbers:

  • 2019: U.S. removed Turkey from F-35 joint production program (Axios, July 17, 2019).
  • 2020: U.S. applied CAATSA sanctions to Turkey Defense Industries Presidency (December 2020).

Turkey angle: Hormuz crisis directly affects Turkey's energy import costs; according to Fidan, Ankara is engaged with Qatar and other mediation channels to resolve it. Additionally, resolution of CAATSA/F-35/S-400 disputes directly matters for Turkey-U.S. defense industry normalization.

What's next: Fidan said mediation talks with Qatar continue and Ankara is doing its part; no new meeting date was announced. The minister also stated that U.S. troop withdrawal from Syria and Iraq is "vital" for Turkey's "terror-free zone" plan, saying it would have positive effects on Turkish security and regional stability; no timeline was given.
