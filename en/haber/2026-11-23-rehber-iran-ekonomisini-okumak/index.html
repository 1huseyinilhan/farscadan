Reading Iran's economy: rial-toman, official/free-market rates, inflation, and oil exports

The rial-toman distinction, official and parallel exchange rates, chronic inflation, and sanctioned oil exports; a guide to reading Iran's economy.

By Farsçadan Editors

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Phrases that frequently appear in Farsçadan's coverage—"the dollar has approached 300,000 tomans," "annual inflation reached 88%," or "oil exports collapsed"—don't tell the uninformed reader very much by themselves. Behind these figures lie three structural features: the same currency having two names (rial and toman), multiple official and unofficial exchange rates rather than a single "dollar rate," and two different government institutions releasing diverging inflation data.

This guide addresses four topics in sequence: the rial-toman relationship and long-delayed currency reform; why official and free-market rates move separately; how inflation is measured differently by two institutions; and oil exports under sanctions. Sources include Wikipedia's articles on the rial and Iran's economy, reporting from Al Jazeera, Euronews, Gulf News, and Cryptobriefing, and Farsçadan's own coverage; every figure is attributed to its source and date.

Rial and toman: two names for one currency

According to Wikipedia's rial article, the rial earned official recognition in 1798 as a coin worth "one-eighth of a toman" and was subdivided into 100 dinars—a denomination now obsolete in practice. The relationship shifted over time: the toman became the currency's everyday denomination among Iranians, and colloquially, 1 toman came to equal 10 rials—a linguistic convention rather than a formal redenomination. In Farsçadan's coverage, prices are typically quoted in tomans because that is how the Persian press phrases daily transactions.

The currency itself has awaited reform for years. According to Wikipedia's rial article, a central bank official proposed in 2008 dropping four zeros and renaming the unit the toman; President Ahmadinejad announced in 2010 that three zeros would be removed; in 2019 a draft was prepared to set 1 toman equal to 10,000 rials, parliament approved it in 2020, but it was never implemented. The most concrete step came on October 5, 2025, when parliament approved a plan—reported in Wikipedia's rial article—for a "new rial" equal to 10,000 current rials and subdivided into 100 "kirans," with old and new notes circulating together for three years. When this plan will actually take effect is unconfirmed in the sources available for this guide.

Official rate, free-market rate: why the price splits

According to a 2019 Al Jazeera report, Iran has operated multiple exchange rates in parallel for years: a government-subsidized "official rate" reserved for essential food and pharmaceutical imports; the "NIMA rate" established by the Central Bank (CBI) in April 2018, through which exporters sell foreign currency earnings; and the "free market rate" (open market), which reflects actual value in daily transactions. When Al Jazeera reported in mid-2019, these three rates stood at approximately 42,000, 110,000, and 130,000 rials per dollar—figures provided to illustrate the system's structure, not current values. According to Al Jazeera, the multi-rate system originated after the U.S. withdrawal from the nuclear deal in 2018 and the reimposition of sanctions pushed Iran into foreign-currency shortage; the government attempted to unify rates in 2018 but the effort quickly failed, forcing a return to accepted parallel rates.

Today, the most commonly cited figure in news coverage is the free-market (open market) rate. According to Wikipedia's rial article, this rate fell to 820,500 rials per dollar in December 2024, the year's low point; by December 2025 it had fallen to 1.42 million rials per dollar, setting a new record—and shortly afterward, on December 29, 2025, Central Bank Governor Mohammad Reza Farzin resigned. Cryptobriefing's August 23, 2026 report states that the free market rate rose from roughly 1.5 million rials per dollar in January 2026 to 1.94 million in July, then crossed the 2 million rial mark; on that date, the official rate was still operating in layers between 570,000 and 1.57 million rials per dollar, a spread of roughly 23%. Wikipedia's rial article notes that the free-market rate had fallen to 2.5 million rials per dollar as of September 29, 2026; a more current, verified figure was not available at the time this guide was prepared.

Inflation: two institutions, two figures

Iran's inflation is measured by two official institutions, and they typically produce different results. According to Euronews' June 30, 2026 report, the Statistical Center of Iran (SCI) reported annual inflation (year-over-year) of 88.6% and twelve-month average inflation of 62.0% for the May 22–June 21, 2026 period; the Central Bank (CBI) reported 83.1% and 57.7% respectively for the same span. The gap: 5.5 percentage points on annual measure, 4.3 on average; Euronews attributes the discrepancy to differences in basket composition, goods/services weighting, and sampling methods, noting this misalignment has recurred in recent years.

Category-by-category price increases also diverged: tobacco rose 173.8%, meat and poultry roughly 178%, dairy and eggs roughly 152%, bread and cereals roughly 139%, furniture and household goods over 111%, and transportation over 103%. In rural areas, annual inflation reached 108.1%, well above the urban 85.2%. The International Monetary Fund forecasted average annual inflation of 68.9% for 2026 (as reported by Euronews)—which does not exactly match the month-to-month figures released by either SCI or CBI; the "annual" figures from SCI and CBI and the IMF's "average" forecast use different methods and cannot be directly compared. The 40% figure appearing in Wikipedia's Iran economy article as of 2026 does not align with these current numbers; the article itself notes as of November 2026 it awaits updating, so it was not used as the basis for this guide.

Oil exports: war and blockade effects

According to Wikipedia's Iran economy article, Iran holds 10% of the world's proven oil reserves and 15% of proven gas reserves; as of 2018, oil accounted for 56% of Iran's total exports of $107.43 billion. The same article reports that during the "Twelve-Day War" in 2025, oil exports fell 94% from roughly 1.7 million barrels daily to 100,000 barrels.

According to Gulf News reporting on June 5, 2026 from ship-tracking firms Vortexa and Kpler, Iran's crude oil and condensate exports fell from roughly 1.9 million barrels daily in March 2026 to 1.34 million in April and to 209,000 barrels (Vortexa) or 260,000 barrels (Kpler) in May—an 89% collapse compared to March. China's Iranian crude imports fell to an average of 1.1 million barrels daily in May, the lowest since January 2025. Floating storage held roughly 190 million barrels at end-April and 147 million in May; approximately 67 million barrels remained "stranded" in the Persian Gulf and Gulf of Oman. Gulf News identifies China as Iran's sole major buyer; no other buyer nation appears in the report. These figures date to May 2026 and may not reflect current export levels; Farsçadan's unconfirmed-claim report on the subject contains more recent but unverified figures.

According to Wikipedia's Iran economy article, the cumulative cost of sanctions over twelve years is estimated at roughly $1.2 trillion; foreign direct investment fell approximately 80% between 2011–2021. The article notes that a U.S. maritime blockade applied in mid-2026 heavily restricted oil exports.

Subsidies and daily life

The daily impact of inflation and currency crisis is shaped by the state subsidy system Iran maintains. According to Wikipedia's Iran economy article, a 2007 reform introduced price controls and subsidies for food and energy; in March 2022, parliament withdrew a subsidy valued at 15 billion dollars in 2021 on food, pharmaceuticals, and livestock feed imports. As of November 2026, each person receives a subsidized quota of roughly 8 kilograms of flour monthly; purchases above that amount are priced at the free-market rate. The same article notes that subsidized goods such as fuel are connected to smuggling.

How it appears in the Persian press

In Persian news, "rial" appears as ریال (Riyâl) and "toman" as تومان (Tumân); prices are almost always quoted in tomans. The official rate is نرخ رسمی (Nerx-e Rasmi) and the free/open-market rate is نرخ آزاد or بازار آزاد (Nerx-e Âzâd / Bâzâr-e Âzâd); the NIMA system is used in Persian as the direct abbreviation نیما. For inflation, تورم (Tavarrom) is the term; "annual inflation" typically appears as تورم نقطه‌به‌نقطه (Tavarrom-e Noqte be Noqte, "point-to-point inflation"), comparing one month to the same month a year prior—Farsçadan translates this as "annual inflation." The word for sanctions is تحریم (Tahrim), which Farsçadan regularly renders in its Turkish coverage.

Turkey angle

Iran's currency and foreign trade crisis affects Turkey through cross-border trade partners. According to Farsçadan's report on land-border blockade, citing Eghtesadonline, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced in October 2026 that Washington sought coordination with Pakistan and Turkey to restrict Iran's overland trade routes; however, unlike Pakistan and Iraq, Turkey did not fully close its overland trade border with Iran and remains Iran's largest land-trade partner. The dollar rate was near 267,000 tomans in mid-October 2026, timing that shows exchange-rate volatility coinciding with border-trade uncertainty.

On the energy side, according to the report in Farsçadan's nuclear program guide citing Daily Sabah, Turkey imported 7.6 billion cubic meters of natural gas from Iran in 2025—13% of Turkey's total gas imports—with an annual ceiling of 9.6 billion cubic meters; that agreement was set to expire in July 2026, and the ministry said as of that report no renewal talks had begun. Every fluctuation in Iran's foreign currency income and export capacity ripples through cross-border trade and energy agreements of this kind.
