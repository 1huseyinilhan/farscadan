How do Iranian elections work? Candidate vetting, ballots, and turnout figures

Presidential, parliamentary, and Assembly of Experts elections: candidate vetting, turnout figures, and 2024 results, with sources.

By Farsçadan Editors

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Farsçadan articles often contain phrases like "the Guardian Council approved six candidates" or "parliamentary election participation hits record low." If read through the lens of Turkish or Western democratic electoral experience, these sentences can be misleading, because Iranian elections operate on a fundamentally different logic: voters control who they vote for, but a body outside the electorate's direct control largely determines whose names appear on the ballot.

This guide covers three elections that Iran's population directly votes in—presidential, parliamentary (officially the Islamic Consultative Assembly), and the Assembly of Experts elections. It explains how candidates are vetted, how stringent that vetting process is, and the turnout figures and results from recent elections, drawing on Wikipedia's coverage of each election, Al Jazeera, and official statements. For an explanation of how these institutions differ and where the Supreme Leader's office fits in, see the guide "Who decides in Iran?"; this guide focuses specifically on the election process and the numbers.

The difference between voting rights and candidacy rights

Every Iranian who has turned 18 can vote. Candidates, however, must first register and then pass review by the Guardian Council (Persian: Shura-ye Negahban), a 12-member body. According to Wikipedia's Guardian Council article, six of its members (jurists) are directly appointed by the Supreme Leader; the other six are chosen by parliament from candidates proposed by the head of the judiciary. Among the council's constitutional duties is vetting the "qualification" (Persian: salahiyyat) of candidates for the presidency, parliament, and the Assembly of Experts—excluding local councils. The council has exercised this power since 1991. In other words, before voters make their choice, this body has already determined which names will appear on the ballot.

Candidate vetting: the numbers show how narrow the filter is

Recent elections illustrate how dramatically this filter narrows the field. According to Wikipedia's entry on the 2024 presidential election, after President Ebrahim Raisi died in a helicopter crash on May 19, 2024, the hastily called snap election drew 80 candidates (four women). The Guardian Council approved only six of them (Ghalibaf, Jalili, Pezeshkian, Purmuhammadi, Qazizadeh Hashemi, and Zakani). This means only about 7.5 percent of applicants made it onto the ballot.

The ratio was even more severe in an earlier election. According to Al Jazeera's May 25, 2021 report, the Guardian Council approved just seven candidates for that year's presidency and rejected 585; among the rejected were conservative heavyweight Ali Larijani, reformist First Vice President Eshaq Jahangiri, and former President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad.

Parliamentary and Assembly of Experts elections show similar filtering. According to Wikipedia's 2024 Iranian legislative election entry, a record 48,847 candidates registered for the March 1, 2024 parliamentary vote; only about 15,200 received final approval. In the same day's Assembly of Experts election, Wikipedia reports the council approved 138 of 510 applicants; former President Hassan Rouhani was among those rejected, with no official explanation given.

In all three elections, voting is direct and by the general population; the difference is in which office is elected and how often. In presidential contests, Wikipedia notes that if no candidate wins an outright majority, the top two vote-getters proceed to a runoff; this happened in 2024.

Turnout figures: the lowest in post-revolutionary history

The 2024 elections saw Iran's lowest turnout numbers since the 1979 revolution. According to Wikipedia's 2024 Iranian legislative election article, turnout on March 1 was 41 percent—lower than the 43 percent in 2020 and the lowest in any parliamentary election since the founding of the Islamic Republic. The same source notes that 5 percent of ballots cast were invalidated. The Assembly of Experts election, held the same day, recorded similar turnout at 40-41 percent, per Wikipedia.

Turnout in the presidential election dipped even further. Wikipedia's 2024 Iranian presidential election article notes that the June 28 first-round turnout was 39.93 percent—the lowest for any presidential election in the Islamic Republic's history. Turnout in the July 5 runoff rebounded to 49.68 percent, with reformist Massoud Pezeshkian securing 16,384,403 votes (54.76 percent) and hardliner Saeed Jalili receiving 13,538,179 votes (45.24 percent).

The results: which bloc won how many seats

Despite low turnout, the hardline bloc strengthened its parliament hold. According to Wikipedia's 2024 Iranian legislative election entry, hardline coalitions captured the bulk of the 290 seats—two separate hardline blocs won 107 and 79 seats respectively. Independents took 39 seats, the reformist-aligned list won 47, and seats reserved for religious minorities came to 5. The number of women elected remained at 14, representing just 4.8 percent of seats.

A similar picture emerged in the Assembly of Experts. According to Wikipedia, the hardline/principlist bloc held its vote share at 65.91 percent, winning 58 seats (down 7 from the previous term). Independents grew their strength to 28 seats (up 24), while reformists dropped to 2 seats (down 17). In the same election, former President Ibrahim Raisi was reelected unopposed from South Khorasan province.

How the Persian press covers it

Persian media uses two key terms when discussing candidate approval and rejection: تایید صلاحیت (Persian: tayyid-i salahiyyat), meaning "confirmation of qualification," signals that a candidate passed the Guardian Council. Its opposite, ردصلاحیت (radd-i salahiyyat), or "rejection of qualification," signals they did not—colloquially, they were "filtered out" or "disqualified." Parliament's official name is مجلس شورای اسلامی (Majlis-i Shura-ye Eslami), typically shortened to "Majlis" in English and "Meclis" in Turkish reporting. The Assembly of Experts is مجلس خبرگان رهبری (Majlis-i Khobregān-i Rahbari). For more on how these bodies fit into Iran's power structure and their relationship to the Supreme Leader's office, consult the guide "Who decides in Iran?"; for examples of the Assembly's political role beyond elections, see Farsçadan's pieces on the Assembly's call on headscarf and U.S. threats and the NPT debate.

From a Turkish perspective

Regardless of which candidate wins, the framework Iran's government negotiates from remains largely fixed, as President Pezeshkian's own statements show. According to a May 24, 2026 NewKerala/ANI report, Pezeshkian told Iran's state broadcaster IRIB that "without the Supreme Leader's coordination and permission," no decision gets made and that all foreign policy steps are shaped within the framework of the Supreme National Security Council and with the Supreme Leader's approval (he is identified in the report as Ayatollah Mousavi Khamenei). This demonstrates that whatever the outcome of the presidential election, decisions on war, the nuclear program, and regional alliances rest with an unelected office—a framework explained in depth in the Turkish-Iran relations guide.

This same constraint appears in Pezeshkian's own initiatives toward Turkey. According to a September 17, 2024 Daily Sabah/AA report, Pezeshkian called Turkey "a friendly and brotherly nation" in his first press conference after taking office, saying he would visit Turkey and aimed to raise bilateral trade to $30 billion. Yet that target too—like all strategic initiatives—ultimately answers to the Supreme Leader's overall policy frame, meaning which office Ankara engages with in Tehran and on which issues depends on whether that issue is classified as strategic or day-to-day management.
