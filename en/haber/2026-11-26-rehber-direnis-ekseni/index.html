What is the "Axis of Resistance": Iran's regional partners and the network's structure

The origins, structure and figures of the "Axis of Resistance" linking Hezbollah, Houthis, militias, and the Quds Force, explained with sources.

By Farsçadan Editors

ShareWhatsAppXTelegram

Farsçadan's news reports frequently mention phrases like "a group close to the Axis of Resistance," "militias affiliated with the Quds Force," or "Iran's regional allies." These phrases do not refer to a formal alliance treaty; rather, they point to a loose, fragmented network stretching from Lebanon to Yemen and Iraq to Gaza, organized differently in each country. The network's members are not tied to a single command structure; each operates on its own country's terms with its own objectives, but they are linked by weapons, training, and financial support from Iran.

This guide explains where the term "Axis of Resistance" originates, which groups and countries comprise the network, through which institution Iran coordinates this network, and verifiable figures on the topic, based on publicly available reports from Wikipedia, the Council on Foreign Relations (CFR), and the U.S. Congressional Research Service (CRS). For Iran's internal governance structure, the Revolutionary Guards (Sepah) guide was covered separately; this article focuses directly on the network's external components.

The term's origins: where the word "axis" comes from

According to Wikipedia's "Axis of Resistance" entry, the term was first used in 2002 in Libya's official news organ, Al-Zahf Al-Akhdar; the newspaper responded to U.S. President George W. Bush's characterization of Iran, Iraq, and North Korea as an "axis of evil" with an article titled "An axis of evil, or an axis of resistance?" and argued that the common denominator was "resistance to U.S. hegemony." According to the same source, the term began appearing in Iran in 2004 in the newspaper Cumhuriyet-i İslami, referring to Shiite resistance in Iraq; around 2012 and after, senior Iranian officials in the circle of Ambassador Ali Akbar Mohtashemipur adopted this language. At that time, foreign policy adviser Ali Akbar Velayeti described Syria as "the golden link in the chain of resistance to Israel."

According to Wikipedia's definition, the network is an "informal military coalition led by Iran operating in Western Asia," and as Britannica notes, its roots extend to the second half of Lebanon's 1975-1990 Civil War. According to CFR, Iran expanded this allied network through the Quds Force by providing training, weapons, and financing "to advance its regional objectives"; experts argue the aim is to increase regional influence and roll back U.S. and allied presence in the region.

The network's structure: which group operates in which country

According to the CRS's September 2024 assessment, the principal groups supported by Iran include Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas, the Houthi movement (Ansarullah) in Yemen, and militias in Iraq—among them Kataib Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba, and Asaib Ahl al-Haq. According to Wikipedia's entry list, these are supplemented by the Islamic Jihad Movement in Palestine, the secular-Marxist Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP), the Afghan-origin Fatimiyun Brigade, and various militias in Syria.

According to the CRS report, among these groups, Kataib Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba, and Asaib Ahl al-Haq have been officially designated by the United States as "foreign terrorist organizations" (FTO). According to Wikipedia's entry list, the network has at various times included Amal Movement, Afghanistan Hezbollah, Zainabiyun Brigade, and certain militias under the umbrella of Iraq's Popular Mobilization Forces (Hashd al-Shaabi); however, as CFR also notes, not all members are equally tied to Iran, and each operates with considerable autonomy in its own country.

The Quds Force: the network's coordination point

According to CFR's definition, the Quds Force, which is part of the Revolutionary Guards (Sepah), serves as "the principal contact point with these groups" and provides them with "training, weapons and financing." The CRS report also states that the Quds Force "coordinates Iran's support for armed groups abroad." The unit's commander from 1998 through January 3, 2020 was Qasem Soleimani; on that date, Soleimani was killed in a U.S. airstrike near Baghdad airport, and his deputy Ismail Qaani was appointed the same day. The Quds Force's internal structure and its relationship with the Basij was covered in detail in the Revolutionary Guards guide.

According to Wikipedia's "Funding" section, this support is financed through oil revenues, taxes, and state-managed funds. According to the CRS, the IRGC and Quds Force are subject to U.S. sanctions under counterterrorism authorities; the U.S. Treasury Department has previously added certain Turkey-based shell companies linked to the Quds Force to sanctions lists (Farsçadan).

The Axis of Resistance by the numbers

According to Wikipedia's main entry, Iran spent approximately $700 million annually on the network before 2019; this figure declined after 2019 due to the impact of international sanctions. Iran's support to the Assad regime during Syria's civil war reached approximately $50 billion in total, according to the same source. According to the CRS report, the U.S. State Department assesses that Iran provides "the bulk of Hezbollah's funding, training, weapons and explosives"; in 2018, U.S. officials estimated this support at approximately $700 million annually. According to the same report, combined support to Palestinian groups (including Hamas) can reach up to $100 million annually according to the U.S. State Department's assessment; this is not Hamas-specific but a combined estimate for Palestinian groups. The CRS report also notes that Hezbollah's stockpile of missiles and rockets is estimated at approximately 150,000, though experts debate how much this figure has been reduced following recent fighting.

Timeline: 1979 to present

  • 1979: According to Wikipedia's section on Syria (citing researcher Jubin Goodarzi), the alliance between Iran and the Baath-ruled Syria was established this year; this marked the beginning of a partnership that would last 34 years until 2024.
  • 1982: Hezbollah was founded in response to Israel's invasion of Lebanon; according to Wikipedia, this group became the network's "most visible member."
  • 2003: Following the U.S. invasion of Iraq, Shiite militias like Kataib Hezbollah emerged; the CRS and Wikipedia report that these groups received financing, training, and weapons from the Quds Force.
  • 2014-2015: The Houthis captured Yemen's capital Sana'a and a broad swath of the country's north (CRS).
  • October 7, 2023: The war in Gaza, launched by Hamas's attack on Israel, led Hezbollah to conduct "near-daily" attacks along the Israeli border, according to Wikipedia; the Houthis launched a missile and UAV campaign against Israel beginning October 19, 2023.
  • December 2024: The Assad regime in Syria fell to opposition forces; according to Wikipedia's "Funding" section, this represented "a serious blow to Iran's objectives in the Middle East." Hezbollah leader Naim Qassem said the same month that the military supply line running through Syria had been "lost at this stage."
  • 2026: According to Wikipedia's current version, following the war in the region, Iran has made the re-arming of its regional allies a priority despite economic and military constraints; for details on this period in Farsçadan's coverage, see the report on militia activity in Iraq and the CIA's assessment of Iran's possible retaliation scenarios.

How the Persian press refers to it

The Persian press most commonly refers to this network using the term محور مقاومت (Persian pronunciation mihver-i mogavemat), which translates to "Axis of Resistance" in Turkish. Some sources use the equivalent concept جبهه مقاومت (jebhe-yi mogavemat, "Front of Resistance"). The coordinating institution of the network, the Quds Force, appears in the Persian press as نیروی قدس (Niru-ye Qods) and is translated into Turkish reports as Quds Force; the official name of the Houthi movement in Yemen is انصارالله (Ansarullah), which Farsçadan uses both as "Houthis" and Ansarullah. For the role of the Revolutionary Guards (Sepah) in this network, also refer to the Sepah dictionary entry.

Turkey's perspective

Turkey is neither a member of the Axis of Resistance nor an official interlocutor with the network; however, the network's Iraq component intersects directly with Turkey's economic interests. The presence of militias close to the Quds Force—particularly Kataib Hezbollah—in areas through which the Kirkuk-Ceyhan Pipeline passes represents one of the security risks that could affect the pipeline's operation through Turkey, a topic also covered in Farsçadan's current reports; the entry of Iraqi forces into Jurfsahkr, home to one of these militias' bases, was resolved through the mediation of Shiite politicians after Kataib Hezbollah's objections (Farsçadan). Additionally, in waves of sanctions by the U.S. Treasury Department targeting logistics networks affiliated with the Quds Force, certain Turkey-based companies alleged to support Mahan Air were also added to the list (Farsçadan). The Houthis' ship attacks in the Red Sea also indirectly affect global maritime shipping lanes of which Turkey is a part (Farsçadan).

Key terms

  • Quds ForceThe IRGC unit responsible for operations abroad and for relations with allied armed groups in the region.
  • Revolutionary Guards (Sepah, IRGC)The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, a military, political and economic force founded after the 1979 revolution that answers directly to the Supreme Leader.
  • Ansarallah (Houthis)A Zaydi Shia political and military movement from northern Yemen, backed by Iran, that has controlled the capital Sanaa since 2014.
